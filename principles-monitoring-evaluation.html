A comprehensive study handbook organized from the uploaded course notes. The source materials are treated as one subject: core M&E concepts, approaches, baselines, targets, indicators, methods, Theory of Change and Action, Logical Framework Analysis, and causal analysis.

How the Notes Fit Together

01 · Foundations

Monitoring and Evaluation: Meaning and Purpose

The notes describe Monitoring and Evaluation (M&E) as a continuous management function used to assess progress toward expected results, identify implementation bottlenecks, and highlight unintended positive or negative effects of a project, programme, investment plan or its activities.

A simple distinction used in the notes is that monitoring is like observing and identifying, while evaluation is like judging and assigning a score. Monitoring follows how an intervention evolves over time; evaluation examines how effectively it was implemented and the gaps between planned and achieved results; impact evaluation asks whether changes in well-being are due to the programme and the programme alone.

Monitoring: an established practice of internal oversight and a continuing function that systematically collects data on specified indicators to inform management and stakeholders about progress, achievement of results and use of allocated funds.

The notes distinguish monitoring from reporting. Reporting compiles, transfers and distributes information, while monitoring includes planning, design, method selection and the regular collection and analysis of information required for reporting.

REAL-WORLD ENGAGEMENT SCENARIO
A project manager receives monthly reports but the project is already three months behind schedule. Explain why reporting alone may not be sufficient and identify what monitoring should have done earlier.
02 · Monitoring

Types, Principles and Steps of Monitoring

Types of Monitoring

Process / Activity Monitoring

Tracks use of inputs and resources and examines how activities and outputs are delivered, especially during implementation.

Compliance Monitoring

Ensures compliance with donor regulations, grants, contracts, laws, ethical standards and expected project results.

Context / Situation Monitoring

Assesses external factors and dynamics that influence implementation and outcomes, including institutional, political, financial and policy conditions.

Beneficiary Monitoring

Tracks perceptions, satisfaction, complaints, participation, treatment, access to resources, equity and experiences of change among direct and indirect beneficiaries.

Financial Monitoring

Measures financial efficiency by comparing actual expenditure with the allocated budget and supporting accountability and reporting.

Result Monitoring

Assesses whether planned outputs and immediate outcomes are being achieved and whether the project is moving toward intended goals.

Principles Guiding Monitoring

  • Inclusiveness: course correction should involve stakeholders and beneficiaries where possible.
  • Credibility: data should be accurate, reliable, relevant and up-to-date.
  • Sustainability: mechanisms and support systems should be sustainable and reflect transition and phase-out arrangements.
  • Transparency: monitoring should be open, honest and accountable so stakeholders understand how data is collected, analyzed and interpreted.

Steps for Monitoring

1
Formulate objectives. Define desired results in clear, measurable terms and develop a conceptual framework for achieving them.
2
Identify indicators. Specify exactly what will be measured for each objective.
3
Set targets. Specify expected levels of results and dates for judging performance.
4
Collect relevant data. Use suitable methods such as surveys, interviews, observations or existing databases.
5
Analyze, interpret and report. Assess progress against targets.
6
Use feedback mechanisms. Gather stakeholder input to identify areas for improvement.

Why Monitor?

The notes connect monitoring with tracking progress, identifying issues and risks, accountability, assessing resource use, providing timely information for decision-making, and supporting continuous improvement through lessons and best practices.

03 · Evaluation

Evaluation, Criteria, Principles and Approaches

Evaluation is the systematic and objective assessment of an ongoing or completed operation, programme or policy, including its design, implementation and results. Its aim is to determine relevance and fulfilment of objectives as well as efficiency, effectiveness, impact and sustainability, while producing credible and useful information for management decisions.

Evaluation Criteria in the Core Notes

  • Relevance: appropriateness of objectives to the problem and environment, including the quality, logic and completeness of preparation and design.
  • Efficiency: how productively inputs such as money, time, equipment and personnel were used to create results, including comparison of costs and benefits.
  • Effectiveness: contribution made by the intervention toward achieving its objectives and intended results.
  • Impact: the broader or long-term changes associated with the intervention.
  • Sustainability: whether benefits or results can continue over time.
  • Coherence: consistency, alignment and synergy among interventions and with broader policies and actors.

Principles of Evaluation

  • Transparency: objectives, methods and results should be communicated clearly and accessibly.
  • Stakeholder involvement: stakeholders should participate to strengthen relevance, ownership and usefulness.
  • Feasibility: evaluation should be practical within time, budget and resource constraints.
  • Credibility: sound methodologies, appropriate data collection and transparent reporting support reliable findings.

Evaluation Approaches from the Supporting Lecture

The evaluation lecture adds criteria commonly used in project assessment—time/schedule performance, cost/budget performance, quality, scope achievement, stakeholder satisfaction, risk-management effectiveness, resource utilization, impact and benefits realization, and sustainability. It also presents evaluation types grouped by timing, purpose and focus, including formative, summative, process, outcome, impact, financial and performance evaluation.

Important distinction: the notes present evaluation from both broad M&E criteria and practical project-management criteria. They should be understood as complementary perspectives rather than identical lists.
04 · M&E Systems

Building a Functional Monitoring and Evaluation System

A system is a group of interacting, interrelated or interdependent elements forming a complex whole. The notes use systems thinking to emphasize understanding the whole through the linkages and interactions among its elements. An M&E system is therefore a structured framework or mechanism for tracking, assessing and analyzing performance, progress and outcomes.

Objectives of an M&E System

  • Track and assess progress toward goals, objectives and targets.
  • Promote accountability through evidence about performance and the use of resources.
  • Generate evidence-based information for management, policy, resource allocation and strategic planning.

The 12 Components of a Functional M&E System

Enabling Environment: 1–6

1. Structure and organizational alignment; 2. Human capacity; 3. M&E partnerships; 4. M&E plans; 5. Costed M&E work plans; 6. Advocacy, communication and culture.

Data Management: 7–11

7. Routine monitoring; 8. Periodic surveys; 9. Useful databases; 10. Supportive supervision and data auditing; 11. Evaluation and research.

Using Results: 12

Using information to improve results—the central purpose of the system.

The notes explain the relationship among these components as three linked subsets: the outer ring creates the enabling environment; the middle ring generates and verifies the data; the inner component turns data into information that informs and empowers decision-making. Without using information, the M&E system is not being used for its central purpose.

05 · Approaches

Conventional, Participatory and Results-Based M&E

Conventional / Traditional M&E

This approach generally follows a linear input–activity–output logic and commonly judges success through task completion and efficient use of resources. The notes highlight that it can be easier and faster to coordinate because fewer people are involved, but it may not fully capture the complexity and non-linear nature of development interventions.

Participatory Monitoring and Evaluation (PM&E)

PM&E involves stakeholders at different levels in monitoring or evaluating a project, programme or policy. Stakeholders share control over content, process and results and participate in identifying corrective action. Capacity building includes developing a common understanding of PM&E, identifying local equivalents for technical terms, using participatory tools and discussing the importance of stakeholder engagement.

Results-Based Monitoring and Evaluation (RBME)

Results-based monitoring continuously collects and analyzes information to compare implementation against expected results. Results-based evaluation concentrates on measuring achievement of outcomes in relation to concrete objectives. The notes emphasize that “results” directs attention toward higher-level objectives and outcomes rather than only lower-level activities.

ApproachMain EmphasisRole of Stakeholders
ConventionalPerformance against preset indicators and implementation logicExperts commonly lead measurement
ParticipatoryShared analysis, ownership and corrective actionStakeholders are active participants; outsiders facilitate
Results-BasedAchievement of outputs, outcomes and higher-level resultsActors align processes, products and services toward results
06 · Measurement Foundation

Baseline Values, Targets and Indicators

Baseline Data

Baseline data is initial information about programme participants or other programme aspects collected before intervention. Baseline values establish the starting point against which future progress can be measured.

The notes identify four key purposes: comparing before and after situations, setting realistic targets, identifying areas needing intervention and resource allocation, and strengthening accountability for changes and improvements.

Steps for Conducting a Baseline

  1. Agree on purpose and information needs.
  2. Decide who will be questioned and the sample size.
  3. Select information-collection methods.
  4. Formulate relevant questions.
  5. Pre-test questions.
  6. Collect and analyze information.
  7. Compile a comprehensive baseline report with introduction, methodology, results, discussion and conclusions.

Key considerations include political, social and cultural context, informed consent, quality control and a representative sample.

Targets

Targets are specific, measurable goals to be achieved within a set timeframe, often expressed quantitatively. They support performance evaluation by creating a benchmark for assessing progress. The notes emphasize that targets should be specific, realistic and achievable, clear, and often quantifiable.

Indicators

Indicators are specific, observable and measurable data points used to assess whether a project is on track to achieve objectives. They provide measurable evidence for tracking progress, informing decisions and demonstrating results.

REAL-WORLD ENGAGEMENT SCENARIO
A clean-water project starts without baseline information but sets a target of increasing household access to safe water. Explain the problem this creates for later evaluation and distinguish the roles of a baseline, target and indicator.
07 · Data Collection

Methods for Monitoring and Evaluation

The notes distinguish quantitative methods, which collect and analyze numerical data, from qualitative methods, which examine non-numerical experiences and perspectives. Methods presented include direct observation, surveys, semi-structured interviews, focus groups, Most Significant Change (MSC) and documentation review.

Direct Observation

Observe what people do to obtain useful and timely information. The notes require a clear conceptual framework, appropriate observers, an appropriate observation type, and agreed collection and recording procedures.

Surveys

Collect structured information from many people. Steps include clarifying purpose, sampling, mode of questioning, question design, pre-testing, and collection and analysis.

Semi-Structured Interviews

Use broad guiding questions while allowing new questions to emerge during conversation.

Focus Groups

Use group discussion with selected participants representing relevant viewpoints; careful participation, questioning, discussion and documentation are required.

Most Significant Change (MSC)

The “story approach” collects and analyzes stories about important changes. The process includes defining domains, collection frequency, story collection, verification, analysis and learning.

Documentation Review

Systematically gathers, reviews and interprets existing documents using clear information needs, relevant document selection, access, extraction tools and analysis.

Criteria for Choosing an M&E Method

  • The need for in-depth insight into experiences, attitudes and perceptions.
  • The need for continuous tracking of activities.
  • The need to understand long-term changes and trends.
  • Resources available.
  • The nature of the programme.
08 · Change Logic

Theory of Change, Theory of Action, Monitoring and Evaluation

The supporting lecture connects four ideas: what change is expected, what actions will produce it, whether implementation follows the plan, and whether the intended change is being achieved.

Theory of Change (ToC)

Describes the logical pathway from activities to desired long-term impact, including the problem, outcomes and impact, assumptions and causal links.

Theory of Action (ToA)

Specifies the actions, strategies and interventions that implementers will undertake to achieve the change described by the ToC.

Monitoring

Uses the Theory of Action to track activities, resources, outputs and progress toward outcomes. It asks whether implementation is happening as planned.

Evaluation

Uses the Theory of Change to assess outcomes, assumptions and contribution to intended impact. It asks whether the project made a difference and why.

The source examples show a pathway such as training farmers → improved practices → higher yields → increased household income, while the Theory of Action identifies practical interventions such as training, improved seeds and demonstration plots. Indicators can then track outputs, outcomes and impact along this pathway.

Difference Between ToC and ToA

Theory of Change focuses on desired change and the causal pathway; Theory of Action focuses on the actions and interventions expected to drive that pathway. They are complementary, not competing frameworks.

09 · Project Logic

Logical Framework Analysis (LFA)

Logical Framework Analysis is an analytical and participatory process for designing, implementing, monitoring and evaluating projects. It breaks complex challenges into clear cause-and-effect relationships and leads to a structured LogFrame matrix.

Phase 1: Analysis / Project Design

  • Stakeholder Analysis: identify people and institutions affected by or involved in the project and understand their roles and needs.
  • Problem Analysis: identify the core problem and map causes and effects using a Problem Tree.
  • Objective Analysis: convert negative problem statements into positive desired conditions to form an Objective Tree.
  • Strategy Analysis: select strategic interventions and decide what the project will and will not address.

Phase 2: Matrix Development / Project Structuring

The matrix is read vertically as intervention or “If–Then” logic and horizontally as M&E logic.

  • Goal / Impact: overarching long-term development contribution.
  • Purpose / Outcome: direct medium-term effect.
  • Outputs: tangible deliverables, goods or services.
  • Activities: tasks carried out to produce outputs.
  • Indicators: measurable evidence of achievement.
  • Means of Verification: where and how data will be obtained.
  • Assumptions / Risks: external conditions that affect success.

Throughout the project lifecycle, the LogFrame acts as a management tool for implementation, monitoring and evaluation.

10 · Explaining Results

Causal Analysis Framework

A causal analysis framework is a structured approach for understanding why a project achieved—or failed to achieve—particular results. It examines cause-and-effect relationships among activities, outputs, outcomes and impacts.

Core Components

  1. Problem or outcome definition: clearly define what is being analyzed.
  2. Cause identification: identify immediate, underlying and root causes.
  3. Theory of Change: specify the expected pathway to results.
  4. Assumptions: identify conditions that must hold for the pathway to work.
  5. External factors: recognize influences outside project control.
  6. Evidence collection: gather data through methods such as surveys, interviews, focus groups, observations, administrative records and evaluations.
  7. Causal validation: assess whether activities contributed to observed changes using approaches named in the notes, including before-and-after comparisons, comparison groups, contribution analysis, process tracing, outcome harvesting, and experimental or quasi-experimental designs.

Typical Causal Chain

Inputs → Activities → Outputs → Outcomes → Impact, with assumptions and external factors influencing the pathway.

Tools, Advantages and Limitations

The notes name Problem Tree Analysis, Fishbone (Ishikawa) diagrams and Theory of Change mapping as common tools. Advantages include better understanding of effectiveness, evidence-based decisions, learning and accountability. Limitations include the difficulty of establishing causality when many factors influence outcomes and the possible cost and time required for data collection and analysis.

REAL-WORLD ENGAGEMENT SCENARIO
A project reports improved school attendance after distributing learning materials. Use the causal analysis framework to explain why the observed improvement alone does not automatically prove that the materials caused the change.

Project Management Monitoring: Detailed Principles, Tools and Types

The supporting Lesson 1 notes add a project-management perspective that must remain visible alongside the broader Topic One framework. Monitoring is described there as continuous tracking of project activities, performance, risks, timelines, costs and outcomes so that the project remains aligned with its objectives. The notes connect effective monitoring with early problem identification, informed decision-making and improved project success.

Importance in Project Management

  • Keeping projects on schedule.
  • Controlling costs.
  • Improving quality.
  • Managing risks.
  • Enhancing accountability.
  • Supporting decision-making.
  • Increasing the chances of project success.
Source warning: the Lesson 1 notes explicitly state that weak monitoring increases the likelihood of delays, budget overruns and failure to meet objectives.

Ten Guiding Principles for Effective Monitoring

  1. Clarity of objectives: monitoring focuses on clearly defined goals and expected results; SMART objectives are often used.
  2. Timeliness: information should be collected and reported promptly because delayed monitoring reduces effectiveness.
  3. Accuracy and reliability: data should be accurate, consistent, verifiable and reliable.
  4. Relevance: collect information useful for decision-making and avoid unnecessary data.
  5. Participation and stakeholder involvement: participation improves transparency, accountability and ownership; stakeholders can include the project team, clients, donors, beneficiaries and community members.
  6. Accountability: clarify who is responsible, what is expected and when deliverables are due.
  7. Continuous improvement: use monitoring for learning and improvement, not only problem detection.
  8. Transparency and communication: communicate findings openly to build trust and support informed decisions.
  9. Flexibility: adapt the monitoring system as risks, priorities or external conditions change.
  10. Evidence-based decision-making: management decisions should rely on factual evidence rather than assumptions.

Common Tools and Techniques Named in the Notes

Gantt charts, dashboards, Work Breakdown Structures (WBS), risk registers, Key Performance Indicators (KPIs), and progress-tracking matrices.

Nine Detailed Steps of Project Monitoring

  1. Establish project objectives and standards: define goals, expected outputs, performance standards and KPIs.
  2. Develop a monitoring plan: specify what will be monitored, how, by whom, how often and with which tools.
  3. Set baselines: establish approved benchmarks for scope, time, cost and quality.
  4. Collect data: use methods such as site visits, progress reports, meetings, surveys, inspections and project-management software.
  5. Measure and analyze performance: assess progress, variances, emerging risks, resource use and quality. The notes name variance analysis, trend analysis and Earned Value Management (EVM). They define EV as Earned Value, PV as Planned Value and AC as Actual Cost; the source extraction displays the schedule and cost variance formulas without readable mathematical symbols, so this handbook does not invent them.
  6. Compare actual with planned performance: identify delays, budget overruns, quality issues and resource shortages.
  7. Report findings: communicate achievements, challenges, risks and recommendations through progress reports, dashboards, meetings and performance summaries.
  8. Take corrective and preventive action: revise schedules, reallocate resources, adjust budgets, improve communication or strengthen quality control as appropriate.
  9. Review and improve the monitoring process itself: improve efficiency, accuracy, responsiveness and decision-making using lessons learned.

Additional Types of Monitoring in the Lesson

Performance Monitoring

Tracks overall progress against objectives, including schedule, budget, scope and productivity.

Schedule / Time Monitoring

Tracks whether activities are completed on time; tools named include Gantt charts, milestone tracking and Critical Path Method (CPM).

Quality Monitoring

Uses inspections, testing, audits and quality reviews to ensure deliverables meet standards.

Risk Monitoring

Tracks identified and emerging risks, mitigation strategies and risk registers.

Resource Monitoring

Tracks human resources, equipment, materials and technology to support efficient use.

Outcome and Impact Monitoring

Tracks immediate results and, separately, longer-term effects on beneficiaries or society.

Source Coverage Addition · Lesson 2

Project Evaluation: Detailed Criteria, Types and M&E System Components

Lesson 2 reinforces that evaluation determines whether objectives were achieved, how effectively resources were used, what impact was created and what lessons can inform future projects. It explains that evaluation criteria are used in project M&E, post-project reviews, performance audits and donor or stakeholder reporting.

Additional Project Evaluation Criteria

  • Time / Schedule Performance: compare planned and actual completion dates.
  • Cost / Budget Performance: assess cost overruns or savings against the approved budget.
  • Quality: assess standards, specifications, defects, reliability and customer expectations.
  • Scope Achievement: determine whether planned deliverables and objectives were completed.
  • Stakeholder Satisfaction: examine satisfaction of clients, sponsors, team members and users, often through feedback or surveys.
  • Risk Management Effectiveness: assess identification, control and mitigation of risks.
  • Resource Utilization: assess productivity and wastage of human, financial and material resources.
  • Impact and Benefits Realization: examine expected benefits, outcomes and long-term value.
  • Sustainability: examine whether outcomes can continue over time.
  • Compliance and Safety: examine legal, ethical, environmental and safety requirements.

Evaluation Types Grouped by Timing, Purpose and Focus

Formative Evaluation

Conducted during implementation to improve performance, monitor progress, identify problems early and improve processes and activities.

Summative Evaluation

Conducted after completion to assess overall success or failure, outcomes and impact, accountability and future planning.

Process Evaluation

Examines how the project is implemented, whether activities follow the plan and what operational strengths and weaknesses exist.

Outcome Evaluation

Measures short- and medium-term results and changes associated with the project.

Impact Evaluation

Assesses longer-term effects on beneficiaries or society and considers lasting benefits and sustainability.

Financial Evaluation

Examines financial performance, cost-effectiveness, costs and benefits, and return on investment (ROI); the notes name cost-benefit and cost-effectiveness analysis.

Performance Evaluation

Examines whether performance requirements were met, practical challenges, lessons learned and recommendations for improvement.

Seven Operational Components of an M&E System in Lesson 2

  1. Indicators: measurable signs of progress or achievement; input, output, outcome and impact indicators are listed.
  2. Data collection methods: surveys, interviews, observation, questionnaires, focus group discussions, reports and records.
  3. Data analysis: process and interpret collected data to assess performance.
  4. Reporting system: progress reports, performance reviews, evaluation reports and dashboards.
  5. Feedback mechanism: use findings for corrective action, learning, decision-making and future planning.
  6. Roles and responsibilities: define who collects, analyzes and reports data.
  7. Monitoring plan: specify what is monitored, frequency, data sources and responsible persons.
How this connects to the 12-component system: Lesson 2 gives an operational project-level view of an M&E system, while Topic One presents a broader 12-component functional-system framework. Both source perspectives are retained because they operate at different levels of detail.
Detailed Source Coverage

Detailed Coverage of the M&E System Components

The core Topic One notes explain the 12-component system in three connected groups. The following details are retained here because each component carries a distinct academic function in the source.

Ring One: Creating an Enabling Environment

  1. Structure and organizational alignment: creates a coordinated arrangement for organizing, coordinating and overseeing M&E activities across projects and programmes.
  2. Human capacity for M&E: concerns the people, skills, knowledge and organizational capacity required to carry out monitoring and evaluation effectively.
  3. M&E partnerships: collaboration among government agencies, NGOs, community-based organizations and other actors can strengthen data collection, analysis and use and improve the quality, credibility and relevance of M&E processes.
  4. M&E plans: provide a structured framework for data collection, analysis, reporting, capacity-building and feedback. The notes identify elements such as indicators, monitoring methods and responsible persons.
  5. Costed M&E work plans: identify the financial resources required to implement M&E activities, supporting budgeting and resource allocation. Examples named in the notes include travel and accommodation, consultants' fees, training expenses and overhead costs.
  6. Advocacy, communication and culture: build organizational recognition of the value of M&E and support communication of findings internally and externally.

Ring Two: Capturing and Verifying Data

  1. Routine monitoring: regular and continuous data collection during implementation to track activities, outputs and progress toward objectives.
  2. Periodic surveys: collect information about people's preferences, thoughts and behaviours using standardized collection methods and sampling approaches that support representativeness and comparability. The notes mention methods such as telephone and paper surveys.
  3. Databases: organize and maintain data so it can support monitoring and evaluation and provide useful information about programmes and populations.
  4. Supportive supervision and data auditing: supervision strengthens M&E through training and capacity building, while auditing examines datasets for accuracy, completeness, consistency and compliance with established quality standards. The notes also emphasize checking validity, reviewing procedures and identifying potential data issues.
  5. Evaluation and research: systematically assess interventions and generate knowledge that can support accountability, learning and the advancement of theory or practice.

Ring Three: Using Information to Improve Results

Using information to improve results is the central purpose of the system. Information should inform future action, either reinforcing an effective strategy or changing an approach. The notes emphasize the need for an information-use mechanism that supports both learning and accountability.

Relationship among the rings: the enabling environment makes a functional system possible; data-management components generate and verify information; and information use turns evidence into improved decisions and results.
Detailed Source Coverage

Detailed Method Procedures and Method-Selection Logic

The source notes provide practical “how to” guidance for the methods below. These details are retained so that the handbook does not reduce methods to definitions only.

  • Direct observation: clarify the purpose and conceptual framework, select appropriate observers and observation type, establish collection and recording procedures, and use the observations to assess implementation or explain results.
  • Surveys: define the purpose and information needs, decide who should be included in the sample, select the survey mode (including face-to-face, paper, phone or online approaches named in the notes), formulate questions, pre-test them, then collect and analyze the information.
  • Semi-structured interviews: prepare broad guiding questions, allow the discussion to explore issues that emerge, select suitable participants, document responses and organize information for analysis.
  • Focus groups: select participants representing relevant viewpoints, decide whether the group should be homogeneous or heterogeneous, use a facilitator and clear discussion questions, manage participation so dominant or disruptive behaviour does not distort discussion, document the discussion with permission where required, and continue until the information becomes sufficiently repetitive for the purpose of the study.
  • Most Significant Change: define domains of change, decide the collection period, collect stories and the reasons changes are considered important, document the context and events, verify stories where necessary, analyze themes and patterns, and use findings for programme improvement and decision-making.
  • Documentation review: define what information is needed, identify relevant documents such as progress reports, meeting minutes, financial records, training materials and policy documents, obtain access, use a consistent extraction approach, analyze the information and report findings.

Criteria for Choosing a Method

Method choice should follow the information need: use suitable qualitative approaches when deep insight into experiences, attitudes and perceptions is needed; observation or related methods when implementation must be continuously tracked; and approaches such as longitudinal surveys when the objective is to understand longer-term change and trends. Available resources and the nature of the programme also influence the choice.

11 · Integration

Putting the Whole Subject Together

The notes form one connected M&E architecture. A project begins by understanding a problem and expected change through analysis and a Theory of Change. The Theory of Action and LogFrame clarify interventions, outputs, outcomes, indicators and assumptions. Baselines establish starting conditions; targets define expected achievement; indicators and methods provide evidence. Monitoring tracks implementation continuously, while evaluation assesses criteria and results. Causal analysis then helps examine why results occurred and whether project interventions contributed to them. Finally, a functional M&E system ensures that information is actually used to improve projects, policies and programmes.

Mastery principle: M&E is not a collection of isolated tools. The strongest understanding comes from seeing how planning, measurement, implementation, evidence, learning and decision-making connect.
