  1. 1A community health project currently has a small grant, several trained volunteers and used equipment, but no plan for what happens when the grant ends. Using the three fundamental sustainability streams named in the course notes, explain why this project might fail even if it has "sufficient funds" today, and describe what each stream would require the project to do.

    The notes state that sustainability is broader than having sufficient funds. It rests on three fundamental streams: programmatic, institutional and financial sustainability. Having a grant covers only part of financial sustainability today, not the whole picture, so the project can still fail.

    • Programmatic sustainability requires that the project's services respond to client needs and anticipate new needs. The project would need to keep checking that its health services still match what the community actually needs, and adapt as those needs change, not just keep delivering the same service.
    • Institutional sustainability requires a strong but flexible structure with accountable, transparent governance. The project would need proper management structures and transparent decision-making that can survive staff changes or external pressure, not depend on one grant or one volunteer team.
    • Financial sustainability requires various revenue sources to support ongoing work and new initiatives. Relying on a single small grant is the opposite of this — the project needs to diversify its funding so that when this grant ends, other sources can continue supporting it.

    Because the project currently has only one funding stream and no evidence of the institutional or programmatic elements described above, it is at risk once the grant runs out, even though it appears adequately resourced right now.

  2. 2A district wants to mobilize community resources to build a borehole, but local leaders disagree on where it should be sited. Using the "Steps to Successful Community Resource Mobilization" from the course notes, describe the sequence the project team should follow before beginning to mobilize resources, and explain why starting with a transparent community selection process matters for this scenario.

    The notes give five Steps to Successful Community Resource Mobilization, in this order:

    1. Start with a transparent community selection process and share results.
    2. Map community priorities and identify leaders through community assessments.
    3. Hold preliminary meetings with community leaders and enlist their support.
    4. Hold community assemblies to elect local representation for programme coordination.
    5. Allow communities to prioritize and select quick-impact projects to solidify support and galvanize participation.

    Applied to the borehole disagreement: the team should not try to resolve the siting dispute directly. Instead it should follow Step 1 first — run a transparent selection process and share the results openly — because this builds trust and removes suspicion that any one leader's preference is being favoured. Step 2 (mapping community priorities and identifying leaders through assessments) then produces objective evidence of where the need is greatest, rather than relying on the leaders' competing opinions. Steps 3 and 4 bring the leaders into preliminary meetings and then into an elected community assembly, so the siting decision is made through legitimate, participatory representation rather than by top-down or informal pressure. Step 5 then lets the community itself prioritize the borehole as its quick-impact project, which solidifies support and participation going forward. Starting with transparency matters because it is what makes every later step credible: if the initial selection process is not open, the leaders' disagreement will simply resurface at every later stage.

  3. 3A toll road project needs financing for both construction and long-term maintenance. Compare the user-pay mechanism and the cost-sharing mechanism as described in the course notes: define each, give one example of each from the notes, and use the notes' comparison table (source of funds, timing, main objective) to explain which mechanism is the better fit for financing a toll road, and why.

    User-pay asks direct beneficiaries to pay fees for services, helping finance construction, operation, maintenance or expansion. The notes' own example of this is highway tolls (along with water tariffs, electricity bills, tuition fees and hospital charges).

    Cost sharing distributes project costs among governments, donors, private investors, communities and beneficiaries according to capacity or expected benefits. An example from the notes is government infrastructure paired with community labour, or donor-funded equipment paired with organizational operating costs.

    Using the notes' comparison table: user-pay draws funds from service users, usually during or after service use, with the main objective of recovering costs through user fees; its typical users listed are utilities, transport and healthcare. Cost sharing draws funds from multiple stakeholders, at various times before, during or throughout implementation, with the objective of spreading costs among partners; its typical users are community projects, donor-funded programmes, infrastructure and research.

    A toll road fits user-pay best: the notes list "highway tolls" directly as the example, the mechanism recovers costs from the very people who use the road (drivers), and the notes state user-pay provides a sustainable revenue source that supports long-term operation and maintenance — exactly the ongoing maintenance need described in the question. Cost sharing could still play a role in initial construction (e.g. government plus private investors), but the ongoing revenue stream for maintenance is what user-pay is specifically designed to deliver.

  4. 4An NGO signs a written agreement with a donor stating that the NGO must contribute 20% of a project's cost as a condition of receiving the grant. A separate community partner verbally promises to also contribute labor, but this promise is never written down anywhere. Using the three types of cost sharing named in the notes (mandatory, voluntary committed, voluntary uncommitted), classify each contribution and justify your classification.

    The notes name three types of cost sharing:

    • Mandatory cost sharing: a required percentage or amount.
    • Voluntary committed cost sharing: written and documented, sometimes linked to disbursement.
    • Voluntary uncommitted cost sharing: verbal and undocumented.

    The NGO's 20% contribution is mandatory cost sharing. It is a required percentage set as a condition of receiving the grant — exactly matching the notes' definition of a required percentage or amount tied to the funding agreement.

    The community partner's verbal labour promise is voluntary uncommitted cost sharing. It is not written down or documented anywhere, which matches the notes' definition of verbal and undocumented contribution. Because it is uncommitted, the NGO should be cautious about relying on it in its budget, since — as the notes warn under cost-sharing challenges — delays can occur when a partner fails to contribute, and this risk is highest for contributions that are not backed by any written agreement.

  5. 5A government wants a private company to finance, design, build and operate a new toll bridge, collecting user fees to recover its investment, after which the bridge will be transferred back to the government after a fixed period. Which PPP delivery model listed in the course notes best matches this arrangement — BOOT, BOO or DBFO? Define all three and justify your choice using their distinguishing features.

    The three models, as defined in the notes:

    • BOOT (Build-Own-Operate-Transfer): the private party finances, designs, builds and operates/charges user fees for a specified period before transferring the asset.
    • BOO (Build-Own-Operate): the private party finances, builds, owns and operates perpetually, subject to agreement and regulation — there is no transfer back to government.
    • DBFO (Design-Build-Finance-Operate): the private company designs, finances and constructs, operates/maintains under a long-term lease, then transfers the asset at lease end.

    The correct match is BOOT. The scenario describes the private company financing, designing, building and operating the bridge while charging user fees (tolls) to recover its investment, and then transferring the bridge back to government after a fixed period — this is precisely the BOOT definition. It is not BOO because BOO involves perpetual private ownership with no transfer, which contradicts the "transferred back... after a fixed period" detail in the scenario. It is not DBFO because DBFO structures operation under a long-term lease rather than the operate-and-charge-user-fees structure described, even though both DBFO and BOOT end in a transfer.

  6. 6A private partner completes a new highway PPP on schedule and on budget, but only about half as many drivers use it as forecast, leaving the company unable to recover its investment through tolls. In a separate case, a construction firm faces major cost overruns and delays during the building phase of a rail PPP. Using the risk categories named in the course notes, identify which named risk affects each project and explain what each risk category means.

    The notes name several PPP risk categories, including construction risk, availability risk and demand risk.

    • Highway case — demand risk. The notes define demand risk as fewer users than expected. The highway was built on time and on budget (so construction went fine), but only about half the forecast drivers use it, meaning the shortfall is in actual demand for the service, not in building it or operating it. This directly matches the notes' definition of demand risk.
    • Rail case — construction risk. The notes define construction risk as delays, overruns or defects. The rail PPP's problem is happening during the building phase itself — cost overruns and delays — which is exactly what the notes describe as construction risk.

    (For completeness, the notes also name availability risk — failure to provide promised services or meet standards — which is not the issue in either case here, since the highway is available and being used, just by fewer people than expected, and the rail project's problem is at the building stage rather than in service delivery.)

  7. 7A company's current ratio looks healthy, but a financial analyst warns that this may be misleading because a large share of its current assets is tied up in slow-moving inventory. Using the formulas for the current ratio, quick ratio and cash ratio given in the notes, explain how each ratio differs in what it includes, and explain why the quick ratio or cash ratio might give a more cautious picture of this company's liquidity than the current ratio does.

    The notes give three liquidity ratios:

    • Current ratio = Current assets ÷ Current liabilities. This includes every current asset, including inventory.
    • Quick ratio = (Cash + Short-term investments + Receivables) ÷ Current liabilities. This excludes inventory (and other current assets like prepaid expenses) entirely.
    • Cash ratio = (Cash + Short-term investments) ÷ Current liabilities. This is the strictest measure, excluding both inventory and receivables.

    Because inventory is included in the current ratio's numerator but excluded from the quick ratio, and receivables are also excluded from the cash ratio, the quick ratio and cash ratio give a more cautious picture in this scenario. If a large share of current assets is slow-moving inventory that cannot easily or quickly be converted to cash, the current ratio overstates how easily the company can meet its short-term (current liability) obligations, since it treats that inventory as equally liquid as cash. The quick ratio strips inventory out, so it shows what the company could cover using only cash, short-term investments and receivables — assets that convert to cash faster. The cash ratio goes further still, showing coverage using only cash and short-term investments. For a company whose liquidity concern is specifically about slow-moving inventory, the quick ratio (and, even more conservatively, the cash ratio) would reveal a weaker liquidity position than the current ratio suggests.

  8. 8The course notes' D Company example shows return on equity declining from 0.22 in 2012 to 0.20 in 2013, even though return on assets rose from 0.11 to 0.13 over the same period. Using the DuPont relationship "Return on equity = (Total assets ÷ Shareholders' equity) × Return on assets" and the financial leverage figures given in the notes (2.00 in 2012, falling to 1.60 in 2013), show the arithmetic that explains why ROE fell despite ROA rising.

    The notes' two-component DuPont form is: Return on equity = (Total assets ÷ Shareholders' equity) × Return on assets, i.e., Financial leverage × Return on assets.

    1. 2012: Financial leverage 2.00 × Return on assets 0.11 = 0.22, which matches the notes' reported 2012 ROE of 0.22.
    2. 2013: Financial leverage 1.60 × Return on assets 0.13 = 0.208, which rounds to the notes' reported 2013 ROE of 0.20.

    The arithmetic shows that although ROA improved (0.11 to 0.13, a positive change), financial leverage fell sharply (2.00 to 1.60). Since ROE is the product of these two factors, the drop in financial leverage outweighed the gain in ROA, pulling overall ROE down from 0.22 to 0.20. This is exactly the lesson the notes draw from this example: decomposing ROE with DuPont analysis identifies the underlying source of a change (here, reduced leverage/reliance on debt financing) rather than relying on the single overall ROE figure, which on its own would only show "ROE went down" without explaining why.

  9. 9The course notes' shareholder example produces a book value per share of $1.00, a price-to-earnings (P/E) ratio of 16.67, dividends per share (DPS) of $0.12, a dividend payout ratio of 40% and a plowback ratio of 60%. Define the dividend payout ratio and the plowback ratio using the formulas in the notes, show how the 60% plowback figure is derived from the 40% payout figure, and explain in plain terms what a 40% payout / 60% plowback combination tells you about how this company is using its earnings.

    The notes define:

    • Dividend payout ratio = dividends to common shareholders ÷ net income attributable to common shares.
    • Plowback ratio = 1 − dividend payout ratio, representing the proportion of earnings retained.

    Using the payout figure given: Plowback ratio = 1 − 0.40 = 0.60, or 60%, matching the notes' figure exactly.

    In plain terms, this combination means that for every dollar of net income attributable to common shareholders, the company pays out 40 cents as dividends (reflected in the DPS of $0.12) and keeps (plows back/retains) 60 cents inside the business. This tells us the company is prioritizing reinvestment over distributing cash to shareholders — it is retaining the majority of its earnings, likely to fund growth, operations or future investment, while still returning a meaningful minority share (40%) directly to shareholders as a dividend.

  10. 10A company's inventory turnover is low and its receivables turnover is also low, meaning both inventory and receivables are held for a long time before converting to cash. Using the formulas for days inventory, days receivables and the operating cycle given in the notes, explain what a "long operating cycle" means for a company's liquidity needs, and explain how the net operating cycle formula differs from the operating cycle formula by incorporating days payables.

    The notes give:

    • Days inventory = Inventory ÷ Average daily COGS = 365 ÷ Inventory turnover.
    • Days receivables = Receivables ÷ Average daily revenues = 365 ÷ Receivables turnover.
    • Days payables = Accounts payable ÷ Average daily purchases = 365 ÷ Accounts payable turnover.
    • Operating cycle = Days inventory + Days receivables.
    • Net operating cycle = Days inventory + Days receivables − Days payables.

    Because both inventory turnover and receivables turnover are low, days inventory and days receivables are both high (since these days figures are calculated as 365 divided by the turnover ratio — a low turnover produces a high number of days). Adding these two together gives a long operating cycle, meaning it takes this company a long time to move from investing in goods/services to actually collecting cash from receivables. The notes state directly that longer cycles indicate greater liquidity needs — the company must fund its inventory and receivables for longer before cash comes back in, so it needs more working capital or short-term financing to bridge that gap.

    The net operating cycle differs by subtracting days payables from the operating cycle. This recognizes that the company is not funding its inventory purchases entirely with its own cash — it is also using credit from its own suppliers (accounts payable) to delay paying for a portion of that time. So the net operating cycle gives a more realistic (usually shorter) measure of how many days the company must actually fund itself, after accounting for the "free" financing it receives from its own credit purchases.

  11. 11A project finance loan for a data-center construction project is structured on a non-recourse basis. At the midpoint of the schedule, the server installation task is only 50% complete when the project schedule expected it to be 100% complete. (a) Using the notes' description of a non-recourse loan, explain what this financing structure means for the lender if the project fails. (b) Using the rate of performance formula (RP = actual work completed ÷ percentage of work planned), calculate the rate of performance for the server installation task and explain what this figure indicates about the project's progress.

    (a) The notes explain that under a non-recourse loan, a debtor cannot be pursued beyond seizure of the asset. This means that if the data-center project fails and cannot repay its debt, the lender's only recourse is to seize the project's assets — it cannot go after the sponsor's other assets or income. This connects to the broader project finance structure described in the notes, where debt and equity are repaid primarily from project cash flow, with project assets, rights and interests serving as secondary collateral.

    (b) Using RP = actual work completed ÷ percentage of work planned: RP = 50% ÷ 100% = 50%. This matches the notes' own worked example for server installation exactly. An RP of 50% indicates that the task is running significantly behind schedule — only half of the work that should have been done by this point in time has actually been completed, signalling a performance/timeline problem that project management would need to address (for example, through cost and schedule control measures such as earned value management, which the notes identify as an important cost-control tool).

  12. 12A new NGO department head wants to build next year's budget quickly by adjusting last year's actual spending figures for expected inflation and volume changes, since the department's activities won't change dramatically. Which of the three project/programmatic budgeting approaches named in the notes — zero-based, top-down or incremental — best fits this situation? Define all three, and explain one advantage and one disadvantage of the chosen approach as given in the notes.

    The notes define three approaches:

    • Zero-Based: built from the unit level up using expected performance for revenues and costs.
    • Top-Down: built from total revenue/expense downward, with components adjusting accordingly.
    • Incremental: prior-year amounts trended based on expected performance.

    The correct fit is Incremental budgeting. The department head is explicitly starting from last year's actual figures and adjusting them for expected inflation and volume changes — this is exactly the notes' definition of incremental budgeting (prior-year amounts trended based on expected performance), and it fits a situation where activities are not changing dramatically, so a full rebuild from zero is unnecessary.

    One advantage given in the notes: incremental budgeting offers a historical perspective and is quick to create — both directly relevant here, since the department head wants speed and is building on known historical spending. One disadvantage given in the notes: trend distortions can arise from full-year vs point-in-time changes — meaning that if last year's figures included unusual one-time events or partial-year effects, simply trending them forward could carry those distortions into the new budget without the department head realizing it.

  13. 13The course notes' wraparound example uses $198,540 in revenue collected from 3,948 eligible visits, measured against a $100.00 per-visit rate used for cost-based/managed-care comparison. (a) Calculate the revenue per visit. (b) Calculate the shortfall per visit compared to the $100.00 rate. (c) Explain what a "wraparound" figure represents in this context, given that the projected wraparound in the notes is $196,260.

    1. (a) Revenue per visit = $198,540 ÷ 3,948 visits = $50.29 per visit (matching the notes' figure).
    2. (b) Shortfall per visit = $100.00 rate − $50.29 revenue per visit = $49.71 per visit (matching the notes' figure).
    3. (c) The wraparound represents the total additional funding needed to make up the difference between what managed-care/actual revenue provides per visit ($50.29) and the full cost-based rate the health center should be receiving per visit ($100.00), applied across all eligible visits. It is, in effect, the shortfall per visit multiplied out across the visit volume, giving the total dollar amount ($196,260 projected in the notes) that must be obtained — typically from a state or local grant/contract — to "wrap around" and fill the gap left by managed-care reimbursement so the health center is made whole at the full rate.

  14. 14An NGO in Arusha needs 50 desks for a community learning center at an estimated cost of TZS 15 million. The notes describe a process of writing specifications, obtaining at least three qualified quotations, evaluating by price, quality, delivery and warranty, awarding based on best overall value, and inspecting before payment. Identify at least four of the Ten Principles of Public Procurement that this process demonstrates, and explain how each specific action in the example demonstrates that principle.

    • Competition: obtaining at least three qualified quotations directly matches the notes' definition of competition — multiple suppliers competing on price, quality and service, rather than an unjustified direct award to one supplier.
    • Fairness and Equal Treatment: using the same predefined evaluation criteria (price, quality, delivery, warranty) for all quoting suppliers matches the notes' definition — eligible suppliers receive the same information and opportunity, and decisions use objective criteria.
    • Value for Money: awarding based on "best overall value" rather than simply the lowest price matches the notes' definition of value for money — an optimal balance of cost, quality, reliability and lifetime performance, where lowest price is not always best.
    • Accountability / Integrity: inspecting the desks before payment reflects accountability (officials are responsible for their decisions and must be able to justify them) and integrity (honest, ethical handling of the transaction) — payment is only released once satisfactory delivery is confirmed, protecting against fraud or paying for goods that were not properly supplied.

    Together, these actions show the example following the notes' stated purpose of applying procurement principles: supporting efficient and responsible use of resources, reducing opportunities for fraud and corruption, promoting fair competition, and helping the NGO obtain quality goods at the best overall value.

  15. 15A donor-funded NGO suddenly needs a specific piece of medical equipment within 48 hours because of an emergency, and only one authorized supplier in the country carries that exact model. Using the Common Methods table in the notes (Petty Cash Purchase, Request for Quotations, Open Tender, Restricted Tender, Single-Source Procurement), identify which method is justified here and explain why the other methods would not fit, referencing their typical use as described in the notes.

    The correct method is Single-Source Procurement. The notes describe its typical use as "justified cases such as emergencies or one available supplier" — this scenario matches both conditions named in the notes at once: it is an emergency (48-hour need) and there is only one available supplier for that exact model.

    The other methods do not fit:

    • Petty Cash Purchase is for low-value, urgent purchases — medical equipment at this scale is unlikely to be a low-value item, so while it is urgent, the value dimension does not fit.
    • Request for Quotations (RFQ) is for medium-value procurement with quotations from multiple suppliers — this is impossible here since only one supplier carries the model, so multiple quotations cannot be obtained, and the process would also take longer than 48 hours allows.
    • Open Tender is for high-value procurement requiring broad competition — this requires time for a wide competitive process, which directly conflicts with the 48-hour emergency timeline, and again broad competition is not possible with only one qualified supplier.
    • Restricted Tender is for a limited number of qualified suppliers — this still implies more than one supplier competing among a shortlist, which does not match a situation where only one supplier exists at all.

    Single-Source Procurement is therefore the only method in the notes whose stated justification (emergency, or a single available supplier) directly matches this scenario.
