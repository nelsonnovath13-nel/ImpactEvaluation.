  1. 1A district health office says it is "monitoring" its malaria programme because it receives monthly activity reports from each clinic. Using the distinctions the notes draw between monitoring, reporting, evaluation and impact evaluation, explain why receiving reports is not the same as monitoring, and state what the office would additionally need to do to be conducting impact evaluation.

    The notes are explicit that monitoring and reporting are not the same thing. Reporting is only the act of compiling, transferring and distributing information. Monitoring is broader: it is a continuing management function that includes planning, design, choosing methods, and the regular collection and analysis of information — reporting is simply one output of that larger process. So the health office receiving monthly reports is only seeing the end product; it is not itself doing the planning, design and analysis that monitoring requires. If nobody at the office is deciding what indicators to track, checking data quality, or analyzing trends across clinics to catch problems early, then the reports arriving are not evidence that monitoring is happening.

    The notes also distinguish monitoring from evaluation and evaluation from impact evaluation:

    • Monitoring is like observing and identifying — it follows how the programme evolves over time.
    • Evaluation is like judging and assigning a score — it examines how effectively the programme was implemented and the gap between planned and achieved results.
    • Impact evaluation goes further still: it asks whether changes in well-being are due to the programme, and the programme alone.

    For the health office to move toward impact evaluation, it would need to go beyond tracking activities and outputs (like bed nets distributed) and systematically assess whether observed changes in community health (such as reduced malaria cases) can be attributed specifically to its programme rather than to other causes.

  2. 2A youth employment project collects data on training attendance, but it never asks trainees how they feel about the training or whether they trust the process, and it never checks whether the project is following its donor's contractual requirements. Using the six types of monitoring described in the notes, name two types of monitoring that are missing here and explain what each would add.

    The notes describe six types of monitoring: process/activity, compliance, context/situation, beneficiary, financial and result monitoring. Two are clearly missing in this scenario:

    • Beneficiary monitoring is missing. This type tracks perceptions, satisfaction, complaints, participation, treatment, access to resources, equity and experiences of change among direct and indirect beneficiaries. Since the project never asks trainees how they feel or whether they trust the process, it has no way of knowing whether trainees are satisfied, treated fairly, or actually engaging meaningfully — attendance numbers alone cannot show this.
    • Compliance monitoring is missing. This type ensures compliance with donor regulations, grants, contracts, laws, ethical standards and expected project results. Without it, the project has no systematic check on whether it is meeting its donor's contractual obligations, which creates real risk of breaching the grant agreement without anyone noticing until it is too late.

    Adding these two types would give the project both a people-centered view of how the intervention is experienced and an accountability check against external obligations — neither of which simple attendance tracking (process/activity monitoring) can provide.

  3. 3A water project delivered all its planned wells on budget and on schedule, and beneficiaries said they were satisfied at the time. Two years after the project closed, however, half the wells are no longer working because no plan was made for spare parts or local maintenance capacity. Using the evaluation criteria in the notes, identify which criterion is most clearly failing here, and explain why efficiency and effectiveness alone do not capture this failure.

    The criterion that is failing is sustainability — whether the benefits or results of the intervention can continue over time. The wells worked at the point of handover, but two years later half of them have stopped functioning because nothing was put in place for ongoing maintenance or spare parts. This is precisely the gap sustainability is meant to capture: continuation of benefits after the project itself ends.

    Efficiency and effectiveness do not reveal this problem because they measure different things:

    • Efficiency asks how productively inputs such as money, time, equipment and personnel were used to create results, including comparing costs and benefits. The project was on budget and on schedule, so efficiency looks fine.
    • Effectiveness asks about the contribution the intervention made toward achieving its objectives and intended results. At the point of completion, the wells were built and beneficiaries were satisfied, so effectiveness also looked fine at that moment.

    Neither criterion is designed to look forward past project closure. Only sustainability asks whether results will continue, which is exactly where this project failed — it needed a plan for local maintenance capacity and spare-parts supply, and did not have one.

  4. 4An NGO has a costed M&E work plan, trained M&E staff, functioning databases and regular data audits in place. However, senior managers admit they have never changed a programme decision because of M&E findings. Using the three linked rings of the 12-component M&E system, diagnose what is missing and explain why the notes would describe this as the system "not being used for its central purpose."

    The notes describe the 12-component M&E system as three connected rings:

    1. Ring one — the enabling environment (components 1–6): structure and organizational alignment, human capacity, M&E partnerships, M&E plans, costed M&E work plans, and advocacy/communication/culture.
    2. Ring two — data management (components 7–11): routine monitoring, periodic surveys, useful databases, supportive supervision and data auditing, and evaluation and research.
    3. Ring three — using results (component 12): using information to improve results, described as the central purpose of the whole system.

    The NGO in the scenario clearly has ring one in place (a costed work plan, trained staff) and ring two in place (databases, data audits). What is missing is ring three — component 12, using results. The notes state that the outer ring creates the enabling environment and the middle ring generates and verifies the data, but it is the inner component that turns data into information that actually informs and empowers decision-making. Because no programme decision has ever been changed by M&E findings, the NGO is generating good data but not using it — and the notes are explicit that without using information, the M&E system is not being used for its central purpose, no matter how strong the other two rings are.

  5. 5A donor insists that the mid-term review of a livelihoods project be led entirely by external consultants using pre-set indicators, with no involvement of the community members whose livelihoods are being assessed. Which M&E approach does this reflect? Using the notes' comparison of approaches, explain two specific ways a participatory approach would change how the review is conducted.

    This reflects the conventional / traditional M&E approach. The notes describe this approach as generally following a linear input–activity–output logic, judging success mainly through task completion and efficient use of resources, and being led by experts — which matches a review run entirely by external consultants against pre-set indicators with no community involvement. The notes note this approach can be faster to coordinate because fewer people are involved, but it may not fully capture the complexity and non-linear nature of development interventions.

    Using Participatory Monitoring and Evaluation (PM&E) instead would change the review in at least two specific ways described in the notes:

    • Community members (stakeholders) would become active participants who share control over the content, process and results of the review — not merely a source of information for outside experts to extract data from. Outsiders would facilitate the process, while the stakeholders themselves would do the evaluating.
    • The review would build stakeholder capacity for analysis and problem-solving, and would build the community's commitment to actually implementing any corrective action identified — something a purely external, expert-led review does not do.

  6. 6An education project sets a target of raising the primary completion rate from 40% to 65% within four years, but no data on completion rates was collected before the project began. Using the distinct roles of baseline, target and indicator described in the notes, explain the problem this creates and why having a target alone is not enough.

    The notes define baseline data as initial information collected before the intervention, which establishes the starting point against which future progress is measured. One of its key purposes, stated directly in the notes, is comparing the before and after situation. If no baseline was collected, the project has no verified starting point — the "40%" figure in the target is unsubstantiated, and there is no reliable way to prove, at the end of four years, how much change is actually attributable to the project versus what conditions already were.

    The notes also distinguish the three concepts by role:

    • Baseline: the starting point, collected before the intervention, used to compare before-and-after situations and set realistic targets.
    • Target: a specific, measurable goal to be achieved within a set timeframe, serving as a benchmark against which progress is tracked.
    • Indicator: a specific, observable and measurable data point used to assess whether the project is on track toward its objectives.

    Without a baseline, the target of "65%" cannot be shown to be realistic (one of the notes' stated requirements for targets), and indicators tracking completion rates over time will have nothing credible to compare against. A target alone tells you where you want to end up; it says nothing about where you started, so it cannot on its own demonstrate change.

  7. 7A programme wants to understand not just whether farmers adopted a new technique, but the personal reasons behind a few particularly striking individual transformations, to use as evidence for donors. Which method named in the notes best fits this need, and why would a structured survey be less suitable for this particular purpose?

    The best-fitting method is Most Significant Change (MSC), described in the notes as the "story approach," which collects and analyzes stories about important changes. Its process — defining domains of change, deciding a collection period, collecting stories, verifying them, analyzing themes, and using findings for learning — is designed exactly for capturing rich, individual accounts of why a change mattered to the person experiencing it, which is what the programme wants for its donor evidence.

    A structured survey is a quantitative method, and the notes describe quantitative methods as useful for measuring the extent of changes and drawing statistically significant conclusions across a numerical dataset — good for answering "how many farmers adopted the technique," but not designed to capture the depth, nuance or personal meaning behind a specific individual's transformation. The notes explicitly contrast this with qualitative methods, which provide in-depth insight and a richer understanding of the nuances behind quantitative trends. Since the programme wants personal reasons behind striking individual stories, a qualitative, story-based method like MSC fits the need far better than a structured, numbers-based survey.

  8. 8Using the course's own youth-unemployment example (skills training leading to improved employability, employment and reduced poverty), explain what belongs in the Theory of Change and what belongs in the Theory of Action, and explain how monitoring and evaluation each make use of these two ideas differently.

    In the notes' youth-unemployment example:

    • Theory of Change (ToC) describes the causal pathway: skills training leads to improved employability; improved employability leads to employment; employment leads to reduced poverty. The ToC covers the problem, outcomes and impact, the assumptions, and the causal links joining each step — it explains why the change is expected to happen.
    • Theory of Action (ToA) specifies the concrete interventions implementers will actually carry out to produce that change: delivering vocational training, providing career counseling, and linking trainees with employers. The ToA answers what will we do.

    The notes explain that monitoring and evaluation draw on these two ideas differently:

    • Monitoring uses the Theory of Action to track activities, resources, outputs and progress toward outcomes, asking whether implementation is happening as planned — for example, whether the vocational training and counseling sessions are actually being delivered on time and within budget.
    • Evaluation uses the Theory of Change to assess outcomes, assumptions, and contribution to the intended impact, asking whether the project made a difference and why — for example, whether improved employability genuinely translated into employment and reduced poverty, and whether the assumed causal links actually held.

  9. 9A rural sanitation project trains 500 households in latrine construction, which designers expect will lead to increased latrine usage and, ultimately, reduced diarrheal disease rates in the region. Using LFA's four levels and its vertical "If–Then" logic, arrange these elements into the correct LFA hierarchy from Activities up to Goal/Impact. Then explain the role that Assumptions/Risks play in connecting one level to the next.

    Using the four levels of the LFA matrix described in the notes, the elements arrange as follows, read vertically as "If–Then" intervention logic:

    1. Activities: conducting the training sessions on latrine construction — the tasks carried out to produce outputs.
    2. Outputs: 500 households trained in latrine construction — the tangible deliverable the project team produces.
    3. Purpose/Outcome: increased latrine usage — the direct, medium-term effect the project achieves once training is complete.
    4. Goal/Impact: reduced diarrheal disease rates in the region — the overarching, long-term development contribution to which the project contributes.

    The notes describe Assumptions/Risks as external conditions outside the project's control that must hold true for the "If–Then" logic to succeed at each step — the example given in the notes is favorable weather conditions. In this sanitation project, moving from Activities to Outputs to Purpose to Goal depends on assumptions such as households having access to the water needed to use latrines properly, communities accepting and adopting the new sanitation behaviour, and no external disease outbreak overwhelming the expected health gains. If these assumptions do not hold, training households (the output) will not reliably translate into increased usage (the outcome) or reduced disease (the impact), even though the activities were carried out correctly.

  10. 10A project distributes free school meals and observes that attendance improves in the same term. Using the core components of the causal analysis framework, explain what would still need to be established before concluding that the meals caused the improvement, and name at least two of the causal validation approaches listed in the notes that could be used to test this.

    Observing that attendance improved after meals were introduced is not, by itself, proof of causation. Using the core components of the causal analysis framework in the notes, several things would still need to be established:

    • Cause identification: distinguishing the immediate, underlying and root causes of the original attendance problem — the meals might address only one immediate cause, not the underlying or root causes.
    • Theory of Change: specifying the expected pathway from meals to improved attendance, so the mechanism being tested is clear.
    • Assumptions: identifying the conditions that must hold for that pathway to work (for example, that hunger, not something else, was the main barrier to attendance).
    • External factors: recognizing influences outside the project's control that could also explain the change — the notes' own parallel example asks whether other factors, such as rainfall or market prices, might be influencing results; here it could be a change in school calendar, a separate transport initiative, or a seasonal pattern.
    • Evidence collection and causal validation: gathering data and applying validation approaches to test whether the meals actually contributed to the change.

    The notes list several causal validation approaches that could be used here, including: before-and-after comparisons (comparing attendance before and after the meals began, while accounting for other factors), and comparison groups (comparing schools that received meals with similar schools that did not, to see whether the improvement is specific to the meals programme). Contribution analysis, process tracing, outcome harvesting, and experimental or quasi-experimental designs are also named in the notes as further options.

  11. 11A construction project manager wants to know whether the concrete supplier is meeting quality standards, whether the schedule is slipping and whether the budget is on track, but has not yet defined KPIs or a monitoring plan. Using the nine detailed steps of project monitoring in the notes, identify which step must come first, and explain why skipping it undermines the later step of comparing actual against planned performance.

    The step that must come first is Step 1: Establish project objectives and standards — defining goals, expected outputs, performance standards and KPIs. Everything else in the nine-step sequence depends on this being done first: Step 2 (develop a monitoring plan) needs to know what is being monitored; Step 3 (set baselines) needs approved benchmarks for scope, time, cost and quality to be set against something; and Step 6 (compare actual with planned performance) is meaningless without a clearly defined "planned" standard to compare against.

    In this scenario, the project manager wants to assess quality, schedule and budget, but without first defining KPIs and standards for each of these, there is nothing concrete to measure "actual" performance against. The notes describe Step 6 as identifying delays, budget overruns, quality issues and resource shortages by comparing actual results to what was planned — but if Step 1 was skipped, there is no agreed standard for concrete quality, no approved schedule baseline and no approved budget baseline to compare against, so any later "comparison" would be arbitrary rather than a genuine assessment of performance.

  12. 12A school-building project team reviews weekly progress reports during construction to adjust schedules and resource allocation. Separately, after the school opens, an external team studies whether student enrollment increased. Identify which evaluation type from the notes each activity represents, and explain the difference in purpose and timing between the two.

    The weekly review of progress reports during construction is formative evaluation. The notes describe formative evaluation as conducted during implementation to improve performance, monitor progress, identify problems early and improve processes and activities — and give almost exactly this example: a construction project team reviewing weekly progress reports to adjust schedules and resource allocation.

    The post-completion study of whether student enrollment increased is summative evaluation. The notes describe summative evaluation as conducted after completion to assess overall success or failure, outcomes and impact, accountability and future planning — and give the matching example of evaluating whether a new school-building project increased student enrollment after completion.

    The core difference is timing and purpose: formative evaluation happens during the project and is forward-looking, aiming to correct course while there is still time to act; summative evaluation happens after the project ends and is backward-looking, aiming to judge overall results and draw lessons and accountability conclusions once nothing more can be changed in that project.

  13. 13An M&E officer suspects a partner organization has been submitting inflated beneficiary numbers. Using the key aspects of data auditing listed in the notes, describe three specific checks the officer could perform to investigate this concern.

    The notes list several key aspects of data auditing that the officer could draw on. Three specific checks that fit this concern are:

    • Checking for errors, inconsistencies and outliers within the datasets — the officer could look for beneficiary counts that jump unexpectedly between reporting periods, or numbers that are inconsistent with related data (such as attendance sheets or distribution logs), which could signal inflation.
    • Cross-referencing data with source documents or external sources to confirm validity — the officer could compare the partner's reported beneficiary numbers against original registration forms, attendance records or an independent source (such as a household list or a community register) to see if the totals actually match.
    • Conducting random or targeted audits to identify and address potential issues — the officer could visit a random or targeted sample of the reported beneficiaries directly to verify they exist and received the services claimed, rather than relying only on the partner's own reporting.

    The notes also mention verifying the accuracy of data entry, calculations and reporting, assessing compliance with data collection protocols, and documenting findings and recommendations for improving data quality — all of which could support the officer's investigation and any follow-up action once inflated numbers are confirmed.

  14. 14A researcher needs to explore sensitive personal experiences as part of a programme evaluation. Using the notes' descriptions of semi-structured interviews and focus groups, explain why a semi-structured interview would usually be more appropriate than a focus group for this purpose, referring to how each method is structured.

    The notes describe a semi-structured interview as using broad guiding questions while allowing new questions to emerge during the conversation. By its nature, this is a one-on-one method between the interviewer and a single participant, which means what is shared stays between the two of them and the conversation can be tailored, at the participant's own pace, to sensitive material as it comes up.

    A focus group, by contrast, is described in the notes as using group discussion with selected participants (5–9 people) representing relevant viewpoints, requiring careful management of participation, questioning, discussion and documentation, and continuing until responses become sufficiently repetitive. Because a focus group is, by design, a shared discussion among multiple participants, anything disclosed is heard by the whole group rather than kept between the participant and the researcher, and the notes' emphasis on managing dominant or disruptive behaviour shows how much the format depends on group dynamics rather than individual disclosure.

    Given these structural differences, a semi-structured interview is generally the more appropriate choice when the researcher needs individuals to speak openly about sensitive personal experiences, since its one-on-one, flexible format better protects the individual space that sensitive disclosure requires than a multi-participant group discussion does.

  15. 15Explain how the Logical Framework's vertical "If–Then" logic and the causal analysis framework's causal chain (Inputs → Activities → Outputs → Outcomes → Impact) are similar. Then explain what causal analysis adds that the LFA alone does not, referring to the causal validation approaches named in the notes.

    The two frameworks are similar in structure: the LFA's matrix is read vertically as "If–Then" intervention logic moving from Activities to Outputs to Purpose/Outcome to Goal/Impact, while the causal analysis framework's typical causal chain runs Inputs → Activities → Outputs → Outcomes → Impact, with assumptions and external factors influencing the pathway throughout. Both describe a logical sequence in which lower-level actions are expected to lead, step by step, to higher-level results, and both explicitly build in assumptions/risks or external factors as the conditions that must hold for the chain to work.

    What causal analysis adds is a structured way to actually test whether that chain held true, rather than simply assuming it did because the plan said so. The notes describe the LFA as a design and management tool — during monitoring it is used to check whether activities are on track using its indicators and Means of Verification, and during evaluation it serves as the baseline for assessing success or failure at the end of the project. But the causal analysis framework goes further by naming specific causal validation approaches for testing contribution: before-and-after comparisons, comparison groups, contribution analysis, process tracing, outcome harvesting, and experimental or quasi-experimental designs. It also separates causes into immediate, underlying and root levels, and explicitly asks whether external factors (not just the project's own logic) explain the observed results. In short, the LFA lays out the intended causal pathway and its assumptions, while causal analysis supplies the tools to check whether that pathway actually explains what happened.
