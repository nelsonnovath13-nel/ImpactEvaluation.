Lecture Overview

Topic Three applies power calculation and effect size to establish sample size in impact evaluation designs. Its main tasks are to determine leading indicators, perform power calculation and effect size, and calculate Propensity Score Matching (PSM).

Study flow: First understand what determines an appropriate sample size. Next learn the formulas and calculation process. Finally study PSM as a method for reducing selection bias in observational studies.

1. Determining Leading Indicator(s) and Sample Size Factors

The notes state that appropriate sample size is crucial for research quality and accuracy. Researchers balance statistical power, economy, and timeliness. Cochran's formulas are mentioned as commonly used for calculating sample sizes for continuous and categorical variables.

Sample-size determination in impact evaluation designs involves considering:

  1. Statistical power
  2. Effect size
  3. Variability
  4. Study design
  5. Significance level (alpha)
  6. Design effect in complex designs
  7. Practical considerations such as budget, time, and logistics

2. Effect Size and Statistical Power

Effect Size

Effect size refers to the magnitude of the effect or difference expected between groups or conditions. According to the notes, a larger effect size generally requires a smaller sample size to detect.

Statistical Power

Statistical power is the probability of correctly rejecting the null hypothesis when it is false. Higher power requires larger sample sizes because it reflects the ability to detect a true effect if it exists.

Variability

Variability is the amount of dispersion within the sample. Higher variability typically requires larger sample sizes to achieve a given level of precision.

Significance Level (Alpha)

Alpha is the probability of rejecting the null hypothesis when it is true. The notes state that it is typically set at 0.05 and affects sample-size calculation by determining the acceptable level of Type I error.

Design Effect and Practical Considerations

For complex designs such as cluster randomized trials or stratified sampling, design effect accounts for correlation among observations within clusters or strata and influences the required sample size. Budget, time, and logistical feasibility also matter because larger samples may be costly or impractical.

3. Sample Size Determination: Issues to Decide First

  1. Estimate the probable problem/characteristic: estimate the proportion of the population with the characteristic; if unknown, use 50% as stated in the notes.
  2. Decide the degree of accuracy: the notes mention 1% to 5%, commonly expressed as 0.01 to 0.05.
  3. Select a confidence level: normally 95%, or sometimes 99%.
  4. Know the population size: determine whether it is less than 10,000 or greater than 10,000 because this affects the formula used.
Calculation discipline: Before inserting numbers into a formula, identify every symbol and confirm which population-size condition applies. This prevents using the correct formula with the wrong population assumption.

4. Sample Size Formula When Population Is Greater Than 10,000

n = Z²pq / d²
  • n = sample size when the population is greater than 10,000.
  • Z = standard normal deviation, set at 1.96 (or simplified to 2.0) for a 95% confidence level.
  • p = estimated proportion in the target population with a particular characteristic; if unknown, use 50%.
  • q = 1.0 − p.
  • d = desired degree of accuracy, such as 0.05 or 0.02.

5. Sample Size Formula When Population Is Less Than 10,000

nf = n / (1 + n/N)
  • nf = desired sample size when the population is less than 10,000.
  • n = desired sample size calculated for a population greater than 10,000.
  • N = estimated population size, such as 1,000 or 5,000.
Step-by-step sequence:
  1. First calculate n using the formula for a large population.
  2. If the actual population is below 10,000, use that calculated n and the population size N in the finite-population formula.
  3. The result is the adjusted desired sample size nf.

6. Worked Example: Nurses Leaving Health Services

The notes give the following situation: the proportion of nurses leaving health services within three years of graduation is estimated at 30%, and a study aims to find causes at a 95% confidence level.

Step 1: Identify values from the notes
  • p = 0.30
  • q = 1 − 0.30 = 0.70
  • For a simplified 95% confidence level, Z = 2
  • d = 0.05
Step 2: Substitute into the formula
n = (2² × 0.30 × 0.70) / (0.05²)
Step 3: Calculate numerator and denominator
n = (4 × 0.21) / 0.0025 = 0.84 / 0.0025 = 336

Result from the notes: the required sample size is 336.

7. Propensity Score Matching (PSM)

Source definition: PSM is a statistical technique used to reduce selection bias in observational studies when estimating the effect of a treatment or intervention.

It works by matching treated and untreated (control) units with similar characteristics, represented through propensity scores. The process estimates the probability of receiving treatment for each individual and then matches treated and control units with similar scores. According to the notes, this reduces bias from confounding variables and allows a more accurate estimate of the treatment effect.

8. When to Use PSM

  • Observational data: treatment was not randomly assigned, such as medical treatments, educational programmes, or policy interventions.
  • Need to estimate causal effects: the objective is to compare treated and untreated groups.
  • Confounding variables: treatment assignment may be influenced by covariates such as age, income, or prior scores that also affect the outcome.
  • Common support: there must be enough overlap in covariate distributions to find meaningful matches.
  • Measured covariates: PSM adjusts only for observed covariates; important unobserved confounders are not fully addressed by PSM.
High-weight limitation from the notes: “Observed” is critical. PSM cannot fully remove bias from important confounders that were not measured.

16. Summary Interpretation for Reporting

The notes provide this reporting logic:

  1. Estimate propensity scores using logistic regression.
  2. Match treated and control units using nearest neighbor matching.
  3. Check whether covariate balance improves; in the example all standardized mean differences fall below 0.1.
  4. Conduct post-matching outcome analysis.
  5. Report the statistically significant treatment effect of 3.9 points, t(94) = 2.87 and p = .005, as evidence suggesting a meaningful intervention impact in the example.

L3 Master Revision Framework

  1. Know the factors affecting sample size: effect size, power, variability, alpha, design effect, and practical constraints.
  2. Choose the formula based on population size and define every symbol before calculation.
  3. Practice the nurses example until you can calculate 336 step by step.
  4. For PSM, remember the full chain: observational data → treatment/covariates/outcome → estimate scores → match → check common support and balance → analyze outcomes → report results.
  5. Never forget the source limitation: PSM adjusts for observed covariates and cannot fully address important unobserved confounders.
