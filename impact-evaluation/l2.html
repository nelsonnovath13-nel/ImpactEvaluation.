Lecture Overview

Topic Two covers research designs used to evaluate the impact of an intervention. Its three main tasks are to explain counterfactual concepts and related concepts, conduct an RCT, and conduct a Difference-in-Differences assessment.

1. Counterfactual Concepts

Counterfactual: A hypothetical scenario that did not occur but is used to reason about what would have happened if a different action had been taken or a different condition had been present.

Counterfactual reasoning is central to causal inference. To determine whether an intervention caused an outcome, compare the observed outcome with the counterfactual outcome—what would have happened without the intervention.

2. Randomized Experiments / RCTs

Randomized experiments, often called randomized controlled trials, are scientific experiments used in fields including psychology, medicine, and the social sciences. Participants are randomly assigned to a treatment group or control group.

According to the notes, randomization helps ensure that observed differences are due to the treatment rather than confounding variables. RCTs are considered the gold standard for determining causal relationships because randomization controls for known and unknown confounding variables and supports stronger inferences about treatment effects.

ComponentMeaning
Random assignmentRandom allocation to groups; typically treatment receives the intervention and control does not; helps eliminate selection bias.
Control groupDoes not receive treatment and serves as a baseline for comparison.
Treatment groupReceives the treatment or intervention being tested.
BlindingParticipants and/or researchers may not know group membership to prevent bias.
Outcome measurementOutcomes are measured in a standardized way to assess treatment effects.

3. Confounding Variables

Definition: Variables the researcher failed to control or eliminate that can influence both the dependent and independent variables, leading to a false assumption of causality or association.

They can create misleading conclusions about relationships being studied.

Methods in the Notes

  1. Randomization: helps distribute confounding variables across groups.
  2. Matching: pairs participants with similar characteristics across groups.
  3. Statistical control: uses techniques such as regression analysis to account for confounders.

4. Quasi-Experimental Designs

These designs attempt to establish cause-and-effect relationships but lack random assignment to treatment and control groups. They are often used when an RCT is impractical or unethical.

  • Lack of random assignment: groups may be formed through convenience, pre-existing groups, or other non-random methods.
  • Comparison groups: groups may resemble treatment and control groups but are not created by random assignment, creating a risk of selection bias.
TypeMeaning
Non-equivalent groupsCompares similar but non-equivalent groups, such as different school classes.
Before-and-afterMeasures outcomes before and after an intervention in one group.
Interrupted time seriesUses repeated measurements before and after intervention to observe changes in trends.

Source Example Explained

A school implements a new teaching method in one classroom and continues the traditional method in another without random assignment. Differences can be studied, but teacher effectiveness or student demographics may confound results, so interpretation must be cautious.

5. Non-Experimental Designs

Also called observational designs. The researcher does not manipulate variables or assign participants to conditions; instead, variables are observed and measured as they naturally occur. The notes mention use in social sciences, psychology, and epidemiology to study relationships and correlations.

Correlational studiesExamine relationships between variables. Correlation does not imply causation but can show strength and direction of relationships.

6. Conducting an RCT: All 9 Steps

  1. Define the research question: clearly define the study objective and specific question; formulate the hypothesis.
  2. Design the study: define target population, inclusion/exclusion criteria, and required sample size.
  3. Randomization: randomly assign experimental and control groups using simple, block, or stratified randomization; use single- or double-blinding where possible. In single-blind studies, participants do not know which group they are in. In double-blind studies, neither participants nor researchers know.
  4. Intervention: administer the intervention according to protocol; ensure the control group does not receive it, though placebo may be used where applicable.
  5. Data collection: collect baseline data, schedule follow-up assessments, and ensure consistent, accurate data collection using standardized tools and procedures.
  6. Outcome measurement: define primary and secondary outcomes and plan analysis for comparing groups.
  7. Ethical considerations: obtain informed consent and ethical approval from an IRB or ethics committee.
  8. Data analysis: use appropriate methods: t-tests/ANOVA for means, chi-square for categorical data, regression for confounders; analyze based on initial group assignment regardless of completion.
  9. Interpretation and reporting: interpret results in the research-question context, acknowledge limitations, draw conclusions, and publish/share results.

7. RCT Example: Complementary Feeding Practices

The source asks whether a specific complementary feeding practice improves the nutritional status of under-five children in Tanzania.

  1. Select under-five children and define inclusion criteria such as age and health status.
  2. Randomly assign children to the new feeding practice or standard practice.
  3. Implement the new feeding practice for the intervention group.
  4. Collect baseline and follow-up nutritional data such as weight, height, and growth rates.
  5. Compare nutritional outcomes statistically between intervention and control groups.
  6. Meet ethical standards, including informed consent from parents or guardians.
  7. Analyze and interpret results to determine effectiveness.

8. Difference-in-Differences (DiD)

DiD is a statistical technique used to estimate the causal effect of a treatment or intervention by comparing changes in outcomes over time between a treatment group and a control group. The notes mention economics, public policy, and social sciences as common fields of use.

Core comparison: In a DiD analysis, compare changes over time in the treatment group with changes over time in the control group and examine whether the treatment group experienced a different change after the intervention.

9. Conducting the DiD Assessment

Step 1: Define Treatment and Control Groups

Source example: a new school meal plan intended to improve children's nutrition.

  • Treatment: children in schools implementing the new meal plan, for example Schools A, B, and C.
  • Control: children in schools continuing the existing meal plan, for example Schools D, E, and F.

Step 2: Collect Data

  • Baseline: before the meal plan, such as height, weight, and dietary habits.
  • Follow-up: after the meal plan has operated for a period, tracking changes in the same nutritional indicators.

Step 3: Conduct the Study

  1. Select treatment and control schools and children.
  2. Collect baseline nutritional indicators from both groups.
  3. Introduce the new meal plan in treatment schools.
  4. After a period such as six months, collect the same data again from both groups.
  5. Compare changes in nutritional indicators between groups.

Step 4: Analysis

  1. Calculate average changes for treatment and control groups.
  2. Use a regression model to estimate the intervention impact while accounting for differences over time between groups.

10. DiD Model Specification and Interpretation

ComponentMeaning
Outcome variableWhat impact is being measured, for example nutritional status.
Time variableBefore/after intervention, e.g. 0 before and 1 after.
Treatment variableGroup membership, e.g. 1 treatment and 0 control.
Interaction termCombines time and treatment to capture the intervention effect.
Y = α + β₁(Time) + β₂(Treatment) + β₃(Time × Treatment) + ε
  • Y = outcome variable.
  • α = intercept or baseline outcome level.
  • β₁ = change over time for the control group.
  • β₂ = baseline difference between treatment and control groups before intervention.
  • β₃ = interaction term and DiD estimator representing the additional treatment effect.
  • ε = error term capturing random noise.

Steps to Run the Model

  1. Prepare data with outcome, time coded 0/1, and treatment coded 0/1.
  2. Use statistical software to run the regression with the interaction term.

Interpretation of the Results (Meal-Plan Example)

The notes apply the model coefficients to the school meal plan example as follows:

  • Intercept (α): Baseline nutritional status.
  • β₁ (Time): Change in nutritional status over time for the control group.
  • β₂ (Treatment): Difference in baseline nutritional status between treatment and control groups before the intervention.
  • β₃ (Time × Treatment): The effect of the new meal plan on nutritional status — this is the DiD estimate.
