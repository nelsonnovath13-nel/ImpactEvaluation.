  1. 1Using the source definitions, explain the difference between "evaluation" in general and "impact evaluation" specifically. Why does impact evaluation require a counterfactual, and what problem would arise if an evaluator simply looked at outcomes measured after a project ended, without considering the counterfactual?

    Evaluation in general is defined in the notes as "a process of judging value based on what a project or programme has achieved, particularly in relation to planned activities and overall objectives." It is a systematic and objective assessment of a project's design, implementation, and results.

    Impact evaluation is narrower and more specific. It "assesses how the intervention being evaluated affects outcomes, including intended and unintended effects." The key extra requirement is attribution: impact evaluation must show that a change can be attributed to the particular intervention, not just that a change happened.

    This is why the notes say proper analysis of impact requires a counterfactual — what the outcomes would have been in the absence of the intervention. If an evaluator only looked at outcomes measured after the project ended, they would not know whether those outcomes were caused by the project or would have happened anyway because of other factors. Observing a change after a project is not, by itself, enough to establish that the project caused the change; without a counterfactual there is no way to isolate the intervention's effect from everything else going on.

  2. 2A vocational training project trained young people in tailoring. Six months later, 70% of those trained had found jobs. Around the same time, the project's design turned out to closely match the priorities of a newly launched national youth-employment policy. Using the OECD DAC criteria definitions and their key questions, identify which two criteria are most clearly demonstrated in this scenario and explain why, using the source's own definitions and example wording for each.

    The two criteria most clearly shown are Effectiveness and Coherence.

    • Effectiveness is defined as "the degree to which the intervention achieved, or is expected to achieve, its objectives and results," measuring whether intended outcomes were achieved. The source's own example states that "a vocational training project is effective if participants gained employable skills and secured jobs." The scenario matches this directly: 70% of trained youths found jobs, showing the intended outcome (employment) was achieved.
    • Coherence is defined as "the compatibility of the intervention with other interventions in a country, sector, or institution," examining whether the project complements existing policies and whether there is coordination among stakeholders. Because the project's design closely matched the priorities of the newly launched national youth-employment policy, it demonstrates compatibility with an existing national policy, which is exactly what the coherence questions ask ("Does the project complement existing policies or programmes?").

  3. 3Compare mid-term/interim evaluation and ex post evaluation as described in the source's table: when does each happen, and what is its main focus? Why might a donor agency want to commission both types on the same project rather than only one?

    According to the source's table on types of evaluation:

    • Mid-term / interim evaluation or review happens "while the project is still under way." Its main focus is to "review progress and propose alterations to project design during the remaining implementation period."
    • Ex post evaluation happens "a number of years after completion." It "often focuses on assessing the impact of development projects after some time has passed."

    A donor might want both because they serve different purposes described in the notes. The mid-term evaluation can steer the project while it is still possible to change course, catching design or implementation problems early. The ex post evaluation, by contrast, can assess long-term impact and sustainability only once enough time has passed for those effects to appear, and it generates lessons for future project designs. One corrects course during the project; the other tells the donor, well after the fact, whether the results actually lasted.

  4. 4The source states that DAC criteria can be integrated into the LFA Monitoring and Evaluation Matrix, with each criterion linked to a level of the logical framework. Using the "Practical LFA Example" table, state which DAC criteria are linked to the Purpose level and which are linked to the Goal level, and give the example indicator listed for each level.

    In the source's Practical LFA Example table:

    • Purpose ("Increase climate resilience among women") is linked to Effectiveness and Relevance. Its example indicator is "% of women adopting climate-smart practices."
    • Goal ("Improve women's socio-economic performance") is linked to Impact and Sustainability. Its example indicator is "Increase in average income of women by 30%."

    This fits the source's general logic that the LFA provides the structure (objectives, indicators, assumptions, means of verification) while the DAC criteria provide the evaluative lens: the Purpose level is judged by whether the intended change is being effectively achieved and whether it remains relevant, while the Goal level, being the highest and longest-term level, is judged by long-term impact and whether benefits are sustained.

  5. 5A project team tracks the number of training workshops delivered and the budget utilization rate every quarter while the project is running. A separate team, two years after the project has closed, assesses whether the increase in women's income has lasted and whether local institutions can now sustain the programme without donor support. Identify which activity is monitoring and which is evaluation, and name the DAC criteria each one is primarily tracking according to the source's three conclusions on monitoring versus evaluation.

    Tracking the number of workshops delivered and the budget utilization rate every quarter is monitoring. The source's first conclusion states that "monitoring mainly focuses on efficiency and effectiveness because it tracks implementation, outputs, timelines, and resource use during the project" — workshops delivered and budget utilization are exactly this kind of implementation and resource tracking, so this activity is primarily tracking efficiency (and effectiveness).

    Assessing, two years after project close, whether income gains lasted and whether local institutions can sustain the programme is evaluation. The source's second conclusion states that "evaluation focuses more on relevance, coherence, impact, and sustainability, especially at mid-term and end-line stages." This assessment is directly about long-term change and continuation of benefits, so it is primarily tracking impact and sustainability.

    The source's third conclusion ties this together: DAC criteria strengthen the LFA by ensuring M&E goes beyond "what was done" (monitoring) to assess "whether it mattered, why it worked, and if results will last" (evaluation).

  6. 6Explain why the source describes randomized controlled trials (RCTs) as the "gold standard" for determining causal relationships. In your answer, describe the role of random assignment, the control group, and the treatment group, and explain how randomization addresses both known and unknown confounding variables.

    In an RCT, participants are randomly assigned to a treatment group, which "receives the treatment or intervention being tested," or a control group, which "does not receive treatment and serves as a baseline for comparison." Random assignment means random allocation to these groups, which "helps eliminate selection bias."

    The source states that "randomization helps ensure that observed differences are due to the treatment rather than confounding variables." This is why RCTs are called the gold standard: "randomization controls for known and unknown confounding variables and supports stronger inferences about treatment effects." Because group membership is decided purely by chance, any characteristic that could confound the results — whether the researcher has thought of it (known) or not (unknown) — should, on average, be spread evenly across the treatment and control groups. This means that if the two groups end up with different outcomes, the most likely explanation is the treatment itself rather than some pre-existing difference between the groups, which is what makes RCTs so strong for causal inference.

  7. 7A ministry of education wants to know whether a new teaching method improves test scores. Randomly assigning individual students within the same school to the new method or the traditional method is considered impractical, so instead the ministry compares one classroom already using the new method against another classroom continuing the traditional method. (a) Name the type of design this is, according to the source. (b) Using the source's own school example, identify a specific confounding risk in this design. (c) Name the three methods the source gives for addressing confounding variables in general, and briefly explain how one of them (other than randomization) could still improve this study.

    1. (a) Type of design: This is a quasi-experimental design, specifically the "non-equivalent groups" type, which the source describes as comparing "similar but non-equivalent groups, such as different school classes." It lacks random assignment; the comparison groups are pre-existing classrooms rather than randomly formed groups.
    2. (b) Confounding risk: The source's own scenario example is almost identical: "A school implements a new teaching method in one classroom and continues the traditional method in another without random assignment. Differences can be studied, but teacher effectiveness or student demographics may confound results, so interpretation must be cautious." So here, teacher effectiveness or differences in student demographics between the two classrooms could confound the comparison — better or worse test scores could reflect the teacher or the students, not the teaching method itself.
    3. (c) The three methods: the source lists randomization, matching ("pairs participants with similar characteristics across groups"), and statistical control ("uses techniques such as regression analysis to account for confounders"). Since randomization is not available here, statistical control could still help: the ministry could use regression analysis to statistically account for factors like teacher effectiveness or student demographics when comparing test scores, reducing (though not eliminating) the risk that these factors explain the observed difference rather than the teaching method.

  8. 8The source's own RCT example asks whether a new complementary feeding practice improves the nutritional status of under-five children in Tanzania. List, in order, all nine of the source's steps for conducting an RCT, and for each step briefly state what it would involve in this specific study, drawing on the details given in the RCT example section.

    1. Define the research question: Does the new complementary feeding practice improve the nutritional status of under-five children in Tanzania? This is the study objective and hypothesis.
    2. Design the study: Select under-five children and define inclusion criteria such as age and health status; determine the required sample size.
    3. Randomization: Randomly assign the selected children to the new feeding practice group or the standard practice (control) group.
    4. Intervention: Implement the new feeding practice for the intervention group according to protocol, while the control group continues the standard practice.
    5. Data collection: Collect baseline and follow-up nutritional data such as weight, height, and growth rates, using consistent, standardized procedures.
    6. Outcome measurement: Define the primary and secondary nutritional-status outcomes and plan how intervention and control groups will be statistically compared.
    7. Ethical considerations: Obtain informed consent from parents or guardians and ethical approval before proceeding.
    8. Data analysis: Statistically compare nutritional outcomes between the intervention and control groups (for example using appropriate tests for the type of data collected).
    9. Interpretation and reporting: Analyze and interpret the results to determine whether the new feeding practice was effective, acknowledge limitations, and share the findings.

  9. 9Using the Difference-in-Differences model Y = α + β₁(Time) + β₂(Treatment) + β₃(Time × Treatment) + ε and the school meal-plan example, explain in plain terms what each of α, β₁, β₂ and β₃ represents. State specifically which coefficient is the "DiD estimator" and explain why it — rather than β₁ or β₂ alone — captures the causal effect of the meal plan.

    • α (intercept): the baseline nutritional status level.
    • β₁ (Time): the change in nutritional status over time for the control group — in other words, how nutrition changed anyway, without the new meal plan.
    • β₂ (Treatment): the baseline difference in nutritional status between the treatment and control groups before the intervention started.
    • β₃ (Time × Treatment, the interaction term): "the effect of the new meal plan on nutritional status — this is the DiD estimate."

    β₃ is the DiD estimator. It is the one that captures the causal effect because it isolates the extra change experienced by the treatment group, over and above (1) whatever change would have happened anyway over time (captured by β₁ for the control group) and (2) whatever baseline difference already existed between the two groups before the intervention (captured by β₂). β₁ alone only tells you the general trend in the control group, and β₂ alone only tells you a pre-existing gap; neither reflects the meal plan's effect. Only the interaction term shows whether the treatment group's change over time was different from the control group's change over time, which is exactly the "difference in differences" the method is named after.

  10. 10Compare a quasi-experimental design with a non-experimental design as described in the source. Does either design involve the researcher manipulating variables or intervening with the groups being studied? Give one named example of each type from the source's tables, and explain what distinguishes them from each other.

    A quasi-experimental design "attempts to establish cause-and-effect relationships but lacks random assignment to treatment and control groups." Groups are formed through convenience, pre-existing groups, or other non-random methods, but there is still typically an intervention being compared across groups. An example is the non-equivalent groups type, which "compares similar but non-equivalent groups, such as different school classes."

    A non-experimental (observational) design is different: "the researcher does not manipulate variables or assign participants to conditions; instead, variables are observed and measured as they naturally occur." An example is a cross-sectional study, which "collect[s] data at one point in time" and is "useful for prevalence and associations," such as studying physical activity and mental health among adults.

    The key distinction: in a quasi-experimental design, an intervention or treatment is still being applied and compared between groups, just without random assignment, so there remains some attempt at establishing cause and effect. In a non-experimental design, the researcher does not manipulate or assign anything at all — they simply observe variables as they naturally occur, which is why non-experimental designs are better suited to describing relationships and correlations than to establishing causation.

  11. 11A researcher is studying whether exercise (the independent variable) improves mental health (the dependent variable), but is concerned that income level might influence both how much people are able to exercise and their mental health. Using the source's definition of a confounding variable, explain why income is a potential confounder here. Then, using the correct named method from the source, describe how a statistical approach could address this without needing random assignment.

    The source defines confounding variables as "variables the researcher failed to control or eliminate that can influence both the dependent and independent variables, leading to a false assumption of causality or association." Income fits this definition exactly in this scenario: it can influence the independent variable (people with higher income may have more time, safer environments, or gym access, affecting how much they exercise) and it can independently influence the dependent variable (income affects stress levels, healthcare access, and other things linked to mental health). If income is not accounted for, the researcher risks wrongly concluding that exercise itself is driving mental health differences, when income could be driving both.

    Since random assignment of exercise levels is not being used here, the appropriate named method from the source is statistical control, which "uses techniques such as regression analysis to account for confounders." The researcher could include income as a control variable in a regression model alongside exercise when predicting mental health, which statistically accounts for income's influence and gives a clearer estimate of exercise's own association with mental health.

  12. 12A survey team wants to estimate the school dropout rate among adolescent girls in a district. No prior estimate of this proportion exists, so, following the source's guidance, they use 50%. They want a 95% confidence level and a desired degree of accuracy of 0.05. Using the source's formula for a population greater than 10,000, calculate the required sample size step by step, in the style of the source's own worked example.

    Step 1: Identify the values.

    • p = 0.50 (unknown proportion, so the source's guidance is to use 50%)
    • q = 1 − 0.50 = 0.50
    • For a simplified 95% confidence level, Z = 2
    • d = 0.05 (desired degree of accuracy)

    Step 2: Substitute into the formula n = Z²pq / d².

    n = (2² × 0.50 × 0.50) / (0.05²)

    Step 3: Calculate numerator and denominator.

    n = (4 × 0.25) / 0.0025 = 1.0 / 0.0025 = 400

    Result: the required sample size is 400.

  13. 13Suppose the district in the previous question actually has a total adolescent-girl population of only 3,000, which is fewer than 10,000. Using the sample size you calculated in the previous question and the source's finite-population formula, calculate the adjusted required sample size. Show your working.

    Because the population (N = 3,000) is less than 10,000, the source's sequence applies: first calculate n for a population greater than 10,000 (already done: n = 400), then use that n together with N in the finite-population formula nf = n / (1 + n/N).

    Step 1: Identify values.

    • n = 400 (from the previous question)
    • N = 3,000

    Step 2: Substitute into the formula.

    nf = 400 / (1 + 400/3,000)

    Step 3: Calculate.

    400/3,000 = 0.1333; 1 + 0.1333 = 1.1333; nf = 400 / 1.1333 ≈ 353

    Result: the adjusted required sample size is approximately 353.

  14. 14An NGO gave a cash-transfer programme to some households, but households were not randomly assigned — they opted in based on need. The NGO wants to estimate the causal effect of the transfer on children's school attendance. Using the source's Propensity Score Matching (PSM) chain from Topic Three, explain step by step how the NGO should proceed. Name each required element (treatment variable, covariates, outcome variable), the estimation and matching approach, and the two checks that must be carried out before analyzing outcomes.

    This situation matches exactly when the source says PSM should be used: it is observational data where "treatment was not randomly assigned," the objective is to estimate a causal effect by comparing treated and untreated groups, and confounding variables such as household need (which likely influenced who opted in) may also affect school attendance.

    1. Define the required elements. The treatment variable is binary: 1 = household received the cash transfer, 0 = did not. The covariates (independent variables) are used to predict the likelihood of receiving treatment — for example household income, parental education, or household size. The outcome variable is children's school attendance, the variable whose treatment effect is to be estimated.
    2. Estimate propensity scores. Use logistic regression to estimate, for each household, the probability of having received the cash transfer based on the covariates.
    3. Match treated and control units. Match treated households to untreated households with similar propensity scores (for example using nearest-neighbor matching), so that treated and control units become comparable on observed characteristics.
    4. Check common support and covariate balance before analyzing outcomes. Common support means there must be enough overlap in covariate distributions to find meaningful matches; covariate balance means checking whether standardized differences between treated and control groups have fallen (the source's target is standardized differences below 0.1) after matching.
    5. Conduct outcome analysis. Once balance is confirmed, compare school attendance between matched treated and control households (for example with a paired t-test or regression) to estimate the treatment effect.

    The source's important limitation should also be noted: PSM adjusts only for observed covariates like income and education; it "cannot fully remove bias from important confounders that were not measured," so any unmeasured factor that affected both opting in and school attendance would remain a source of bias.

  15. 15The source's covariate balance table shows standardized differences before and after matching (for example, Age: 0.45 before matching and 0.05 after, with p = 0.03 before and p = 0.72 after). Explain what this pattern indicates about selection bias before matching, what threshold the source uses to judge "good balance," and why the source describes a well-balanced matched sample as creating a "pseudo-randomized comparison group." Then, using the outcome analysis results (mean difference 3.9, t(94) = 2.87, p = .005), explain what conclusion the notes draw about the treatment effect.

    Before matching, the standardized difference for age (0.45) is well above the source's threshold, and its p-value (0.03) is statistically significant — meaning treated and control groups differed meaningfully on age (and similarly on income and, to a lesser extent, gender) before any matching was done. The source explains that differences above 0.1 "suggest imbalance and selection bias": the treated and control groups were systematically different on observed characteristics before matching, so a simple comparison between them would risk confusing those pre-existing differences with the effect of the treatment.

    The source's goal is standardized differences below 0.1 after matching. In the example, after matching all shown covariates (age 0.05, income 0.08, gender 0.02) fall below 0.1, with non-significant p-values (0.72, 0.65, 0.90). This is why the source describes a well-balanced matched sample as a "pseudo-randomized comparison group": once treated and control units are statistically similar on observed covariates, the comparison behaves somewhat like it would if the groups had been formed by random assignment, even though the original data was observational.

    Using the outcome results — treated group mean 78.2 versus control group mean 74.3, a mean difference of 3.9, t(94) = 2.87, p = .005 — the source states that this "tests whether treatment had a statistically significant effect after balancing covariates." Because p = .005 is significant at the 0.05 level, the notes interpret this as "suggesting a positive treatment effect" and, in the reporting summary, as "evidence suggesting a meaningful intervention impact" after controlling for confounders through matching.
