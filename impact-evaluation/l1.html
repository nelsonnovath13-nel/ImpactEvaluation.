Lecture Overview

Topic One establishes the foundation of the course. It begins with the general meaning of evaluation, narrows to impact evaluation, explains when evaluations can take place, introduces the six revised OECD DAC criteria, and then connects those criteria to the Logical Framework Approach (LFA) Monitoring and Evaluation Matrix.

Study connection: The lecture moves from the broad question “How do we judge a project?” to the more specific question “What changes can be attributed to an intervention?” The DAC criteria then provide different lenses for judging project performance, while the LFA matrix provides a structure for organizing objectives, indicators, assumptions, and evidence.

1. What Is Evaluation?

Source definition: Evaluation is a process of judging value based on what a project or programme has achieved, particularly in relation to planned activities and overall objectives.

The notes also describe programme/project evaluation as a systematic and objective assessment of ongoing or completed projects or programmes in terms of their design, implementation, and results.

How to understand the three areas

  1. Design: Look at how the project or programme was planned.
  2. Implementation: Examine how the planned work was carried out.
  3. Results: Assess what the project or programme achieved.

REAL-WORLD ENGAGEMENT SCENARIO

A project planned to train farmers, distribute materials, and improve production. An evaluation can examine whether the project was designed appropriately, whether activities were implemented as planned, and what results were achieved.

  1. Which part concerns project design?
  2. Which part concerns implementation?
  3. Which part concerns results?

2. What Is Impact Evaluation?

Source definition: Impact evaluation assesses how the intervention being evaluated affects outcomes, including intended and unintended effects.

The notes emphasize that proper analysis of impact requires a counterfactual: what those outcomes would have been in the absence of the intervention. Impact evaluation therefore assesses changes that can be attributed to a particular intervention, such as a project, programme, or policy, including intended changes and, ideally, unintended changes.

Study explanation: The word attributed is important in this lecture. Observing a change after a project is not by itself enough to establish that the project caused the change. The source introduces the counterfactual because impact evaluation needs a way to think about what would have happened without the intervention.

3. Types of Evaluation

The notes organize evaluation by the stage at which it is conducted. Evaluation can take place during implementation, at the end, or after completion. It may help steer the project or generate lessons for future projects and programming.

4. Key Aspects (Criteria) of Evaluation

The OECD Development Assistance Committee (DAC) evaluation criteria are internationally recognized dimensions used to assess the performance and results of projects, programmes, and policies, especially in development and humanitarian interventions. The revised 2020 criteria in the notes are:

  1. Relevance
  2. Coherence
  3. Effectiveness
  4. Efficiency
  5. Impact
  6. Sustainability
Important distinction: These criteria are not six identical questions. Each criterion examines project performance from a different angle. The following sections should therefore be studied separately before connecting them.

5. Relevance

Definition: The extent to which the objectives and design of the intervention are consistent with beneficiaries’ needs, country priorities, and institutional policies.

It assesses how well a project responds to the needs, priorities, and context of beneficiaries and stakeholders.

Key Questions from the Notes

  • Is the project addressing the actual problem?
  • Are the objectives aligned with beneficiary priorities?
  • Is the intervention appropriate to the context?

Example: A climate adaptation project introducing drought-resistant crops in drought-prone communities would be relevant if it addresses local agricultural challenges.

6. Coherence

Definition: The compatibility of the intervention with other interventions in a country, sector, or institution.

This criterion examines compatibility with other policies, programmes, and interventions.

  • Does the project complement existing policies or programmes?
  • Are there conflicts or duplication with other interventions?
  • Is there coordination among stakeholders?

Example: A women’s empowerment project aligned with national gender policies and local NGO activities demonstrates coherence.

7. Effectiveness

Definition: The degree to which the intervention achieved, or is expected to achieve, its objectives and results.

It measures the extent to which project objectives and intended outcomes are achieved.

  • Were the intended outcomes achieved?
  • What factors supported or hindered achievement?
  • Did beneficiaries experience the expected changes?

Example: A vocational training project is effective if participants gained employable skills and secured jobs.

8. Efficiency

Definition: The extent to which the intervention delivers results in an economic and timely way.

The notes explain efficiency as assessing how economically resources such as time, money, and personnel are converted into results.

  • Were resources used optimally?
  • Was the project completed on time and within budget?
  • Could similar results have been achieved at lower cost?

Example: A health campaign reaching many communities within budget and schedule demonstrates efficiency.

9. Impact

Definition: The extent to which the intervention has generated or is expected to generate significant long-term effects.

Impact examines broader, long-term positive or negative effects, whether intended or unintended.

  • What long-term changes occurred?
  • Were there unintended consequences?
  • Did the intervention improve livelihoods or systems?

Example: A climate action initiative leading to sustained increases in women’s income and community resilience reflects impact.

10. Sustainability

Sustainability considers whether project benefits are likely to continue after funding or external support ends.

Definition: The extent to which net benefits of the intervention continue or are likely to continue over time.

  • Will benefits continue after donor withdrawal?
  • Are local institutions capable of maintaining outcomes?
  • Is there financial, social, or environmental sustainability?

Example: A water project managed and maintained by trained local committees after donor exit demonstrates sustainability.

11. Relating DAC Criteria to the LFA Monitoring and Evaluation Matrix

The notes state that the DAC evaluation criteria can be directly integrated into the Logical Framework Approach (LFA) M&E Matrix because both are designed to assess project performance.

The source relationship: The LFA provides the structure—objectives, indicators, assumptions, and means of verification—while the DAC criteria provide the evaluative lens through which project success is judged.

The relationship can be understood by linking each DAC dimension to different levels of the logical framework: inputs, activities, outputs, outcomes, and impact.

Step-by-step way to study this:
  1. Identify the project level in the LFA.
  2. Identify the indicator and evidence used to examine that level.
  3. Ask which DAC criterion provides the appropriate evaluative question.

13. Practical Logic: Monitoring and Evaluation in the LFA

The notes give three key conclusions:

  1. Monitoring mainly focuses on efficiency and effectiveness because it tracks implementation, outputs, timelines, and resource use during the project.
  2. Evaluation focuses more on relevance, coherence, impact, and sustainability, especially at mid-term and end-line stages.
  3. DAC criteria strengthen the analytical quality of the LFA by ensuring that M&E goes beyond “what was done” to assess “whether it mattered, why it worked, and if results will last.”

L1 Master Revision Framework

  1. Start broad: Define evaluation and identify design, implementation, and results.
  2. Move to impact: Explain attribution, intended/unintended effects, and the counterfactual.
  3. Know timing: Mid-term, final, and ex post evaluation.
  4. Master all six DAC criteria: Do not confuse relevance, effectiveness, efficiency, impact, and sustainability.
  5. Connect to LFA: Understand how structure, indicators, assumptions, verification, and evaluative criteria work together.
