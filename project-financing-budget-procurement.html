LECTURE 2

Lecture 2: Resource Mobilization Techniques to Facilitate Project Financing

Concept of Resource Mobilization in Projects

Resource mobilization identifies resources essential for the development, implementation and continuation of work needed to achieve a project's mission. It includes expanding relationships with resource providers and developing skills, knowledge and capacity for proper resource use. It is broader than money: knowledge, human skills, equipment and other resources can contribute to the mission. It also involves seeking new sources while making right and maximum use of available resources.

Related Concepts

The notes describe resource mobilization as activities for securing new and additional resources, maximizing existing resources, and acquiring the means needed for a project's action plan through multiple partners and mechanisms.

ComponentMeaning
ResourcesElements necessary for running a project.
MechanismsMeans that make it possible to obtain resources directly.
PartnersPersons or institutions providing resources.

Importance and Sustainability

  • Ensures continuation of service provision to clients.
  • Supports project sustainability.
  • Allows improvement and scale-up of existing products and services.
  • Projects in both public and private sectors must generate new business to stay active.

Sustainability is broader than sufficient funds. The three fundamental streams are programmatic sustainability (services respond to client needs and anticipate new needs), institutional sustainability (a strong but flexible structure with accountable, transparent governance), and financial sustainability (various revenue sources support ongoing work and new initiatives).

Features

  • Resource identification
  • Identification of resource providers
  • Identification of mechanisms for receiving resources
  • Expansion of relationships with resource providers
  • Right use of resources
  • Knowledge and skills for resource mobilization
  • Human skills, services, information and equipment
  • Seeking new resources
  • Consideration of project sustainability
  • Lowering project risks

Process

  1. Identify objectives of resource mobilization.
  2. Prepare a resource mobilization plan.
  3. Prepare agreements or obtain approval.
  4. Implement the resource mobilization plan.
  5. Monitor and evaluate resource mobilization efforts.
  6. Adjust the mobilization plan.

Types of Resources

TypeSource-supported explanation
Money/CashUsed for goods, salaries and new project work; possible sources include donations, sponsors, government and others.
Technical Assistance/CooperationMay involve skilled employees for a special project or fixed period and may include training.
Human ResourcesPersonnel required to fulfil project roles and work.
Physical GoodsOffice tools, furniture, training tools, raw goods, vehicles and other machines.
Services and FacilitiesA distinct resource category listed in the notes.

Resource Mobilization Cycle

  1. Needs analysis and planning
  2. Donor research/cultivation
  3. Proposal writing
  4. Reporting and stewardship

Needs Analysis

  • What is the problem or need?
  • Who does it affect and where?
  • How many are affected and how big is the problem?
  • Why has it happened?
  • What can be done about it?
  • What will happen if nothing is done?
  • How much funding is needed?

Resource Gaps and Reflection

The notes include a resource mobilization gaps/needs exercise and ask the learner to reflect on future fundraising, what funds will accomplish, what the timeline says about financial conditions, restricted versus unrestricted funds, why resource gaps matter, and what happens if funds are not received.

Donor Cultivation and Research

Donor cultivation means getting to know the donor. Potential sources include multilateral, bilateral, government, NGO and corporate-sector donors. When choosing a donor, consider the link to the project, interest in the programme and capacity to contribute.

  • Research current interests, policies and priorities.
  • Review official policies, reports and statements.
  • Study grant-making strategies and streams.
  • Determine local, national or regional reach.
  • Consider whether consortium arrangements are preferred.
  • Review where the donor funds and past funding.
  • Check for a country officer.
  • Refine the project idea to match the donor focus and ask how the project advances the donor agenda.
  • Before approaching: know deadlines, legal requirements, application formats, information requirements, evaluation requirements, contact points and other specific requirements.

Proposal, Reporting and Stewardship

A funding proposal forms the basis of the donor relationship and should demonstrate careful thought, planning, thoroughness and commitment.

  • Plan the project before writing.
  • Understand all questions and sections.
  • Address technical details and overall persuasiveness, logic and flow.
  • Allow enough writing time.
  • Involve technical, finance and resource-mobilization personnel.
  • Develop a matching budget.

Reporting demonstrates programme results and fiscal accountability, using quantitative reporting (financial reports and impact numbers), qualitative narrative/storytelling, and compliance with timelines and donor requirements. Donor stewardship is ongoing relationship management: bring donors closer to results, nurture decision-maker and organizational relationships, and seek information and opportunities to renew support.

Engagement scenario: A project has volunteers, equipment and a small grant but needs to expand services. Identify the resource types, gaps, suitable donor research questions and the next steps in the resource mobilization cycle.
LECTURE 3

Lecture 3: Prepare Community Resource Mobilization Scheme

Concept of Community Resource Mobilization

Community resource mobilization obtains resources from providers through different mechanisms to implement and achieve predetermined community goals. It seeks needed resources in a timely and cost-effective way, with the right type at the right time and price, followed by proper use and optimum utilization. It also secures new resources while maximizing existing ones.

The notes describe it as bringing human and non-human resources together for developmental activities and sustainable development. It can be stimulated by the community itself or others but is planned, carried out and evaluated on a participatory and sustained basis. It also creates dialogue about who decides what and how, allowing people to participate in decisions affecting their lives.

Need and Significance

  • Continuation of community service provision
  • Community sustainability
  • Improvement and scale-up of products and services
  • Seeking more resources to help more people
  • High ethical and behavioural standards
  • Accountability to beneficiaries and donors
  • Improved quality, operational efficiency and programme effectiveness
  • Greater creativity and capacity in fundraising, events and resource generation
  • Organisations, both in the public and private sector, must be in the community on generating new business to stay in the community.

Features of Community Resource Mobilization

  • Resource identification
  • Identification of resource provider
  • Identification of mechanism to receive resource
  • Expansion of relations with the resource provider
  • Right use of resource
  • Knowledge and skills to resource mobilization
  • Human skills, service, information, equipment
  • Seeking out new resource
  • Thought of institutional sustainability
  • Lower financial risk

Importance

  • Diversify and expand resources.
  • Support an independent budget and reduce dependence on programmes chosen only by donor agencies.
  • Decrease dependency on others and lessen the risk of becoming contractors of foreign donor agencies.
  • Support sustainability and maximum use of domestic capital and skills.
  • Deepen relationships with stakeholders and communities.
  • Improve the community image and relationships.
  • Fulfil responsibilities to the community.
  • Run programmes based on genuine community needs and advocate for them.
  • Disseminate good practices, develop new thinking, challenge old traditions and enhance community dignity.

Steps to Successful Community Resource Mobilization

  1. Start with a transparent community selection process and share results.
  2. Map community priorities and identify leaders through community assessments.
  3. Hold preliminary meetings with community leaders and enlist their support.
  4. Hold community assemblies to elect local representation for programme coordination.
  5. Allow communities to prioritize and select quick-impact projects to solidify support and galvanize participation.

Required Analytical and Supportive Resources

  • Leadership
  • Community capacity
  • Communication channels
  • Assessments
  • Problem solving
  • Resource mobilization
  • Administrative and operational management

Fourteen Strategies

  1. Secure strong leadership
  2. Establish a formal structure
  3. Engage diverse community groups, leaders and residents
  4. Ensure authentic participation and shared decision-making
  5. Ensure authentic and productive roles for young people
  6. Develop a shared vision
  7. Conduct a needs assessment
  8. Create a strategic plan
  9. Implement mutually reinforcing strategies
  10. Create a fundraising strategy
  11. Establish effective internal communication channels
  12. Educate the community
  13. Conduct process and outcome evaluations
  14. Evaluate the community mobilization effort separately

Community Resource Mobilization Scheme

The scheme is a blueprint and roadmap for mobilization. Its overall objective is a clear, systematic, predictable and coordinated approach to soliciting, acquiring, using, managing, reporting, monitoring and evaluating assistance from International Cooperating Partners while expanding the resource base for sustainable availability.

Specific Objectives

  • Provide a coordinated approach to soliciting, acquiring, using, monitoring and managing financial inflows and development-cooperation support.
  • Enhance effectiveness of development assistance in community projects.
  • Improve relations and dialogue between projects and International Cooperating Partners.
  • Improve structures and systems for better resource management.
  • Align partner support with priority areas, systems, structures and procedures.
  • Increase domestic resource mobilization and broaden channels through alternative sources to reduce dependency and strengthen mobilization.

Guiding Principles

  • Meet community needs.
  • Promote efficiency and effectiveness.
  • Build on national, regional and international synergies.
  • Strengthen capacity: capacity building is continuous and projects/programmes should include components that strengthen institutional and human-resource capacity to generate and monitor funding.
Engagement scenario: A community wants to improve water access but local leaders disagree on priorities. Apply the participation steps, strategies and guiding principles before deciding how resources should be mobilized.
LECTURE 3A

Lecture 3a: Tapping External Funds and Cost Sharing

Tapping External Sources of Funds

This involves researching funding organizations that align with project priorities and demonstrating impact and feasibility.

Funding Sources Listed in the Notes

  • Government agencies and local authorities
  • Private foundations
  • Nonprofit organizations and grant programmes
  • Corporate sponsorships
  • Crowdfunding platforms
  • Commercial bank loans
  • Private investors, including venture capitalists and angel investors
  • Charities
  • Peer-to-peer lending and other alternative options
  • Public–private partnerships
  • Development partners and international organizations

Process of Tapping External Funds

  1. Understand funder requirements, guidelines and eligibility criteria.
  2. Highlight alignment with priorities, mission and values.
  3. Demonstrate potential impact on communities or stakeholders.
  4. Present a feasible timeline, budget and project scope.
  5. Write a strong narrative explaining purpose, problem and community benefit.

Steps to Secure External Funding

  1. Define objectives, scope and expected outcomes.
  2. Prepare a detailed proposal and budget.
  3. Identify sources whose priorities match the project.
  4. Submit a strong application tailored to each funder.
  5. Demonstrate feasibility, sustainability and expected impact.
  6. Establish sound financial management and reporting systems to build confidence.

User-Pay and Cost-Sharing Mechanisms

A user-pay mechanism asks direct beneficiaries to pay fees for services, helping finance construction, operation, maintenance or expansion. Examples include highway tolls, water tariffs, electricity bills, tuition fees and hospital charges.

User-pay advantagesUser-pay challenges
Sustainable revenue source; efficient service use; less government dependence; supports long-term operation and maintenance.High fees may limit access for low-income users; revenue may fluctuate with demand; efficient billing and collection are required.

Cost sharing distributes project costs among governments, donors, private investors, communities and beneficiaries according to capacity or expected benefits. Examples include government infrastructure plus community labour, donor-funded equipment plus organizational operating costs, subsidized irrigation contributions and jointly financed PPPs.

Cost-sharing advantagesCost-sharing challenges
Reduces burden on one party; encourages ownership and commitment; increases available resources; enhances sustainability.Requires clear contribution agreements; coordination can be complex; delays can occur when a partner fails to contribute.

Cost-Sharing Considerations and Types

  • Compliance with funding-agency and organizational regulations.
  • Documentation of cash, in-kind resources and personnel effort.
  • Clear communication of duties, roles, responsibilities and agreement terms.
  • Mandatory cost sharing: required percentage or amount.
  • Voluntary committed cost sharing: written and documented, sometimes linked to disbursement.
  • Voluntary uncommitted cost sharing: verbal and undocumented.
AspectUser-PayCost Sharing
Source of fundsService usersMultiple stakeholders
TimingUsually during or after service useBefore, during or throughout implementation
Main objectiveRecover costs through user feesSpread costs among partners
Typical usersUtilities, transport, healthcareCommunity projects, donor-funded programmes, infrastructure, research
LECTURE 5

Lecture 5: Prepare Public–Private Partnership Arrangement

Meaning and Rationale

A public–private partnership (PPP) is collaboration between a government agency and a private-sector company to finance, build and operate projects. Financing through PPP may allow projects to be completed sooner or become possible where they otherwise would not. Arrangements can involve concessions of tax or operating revenue, liability protection or partial ownership rights over public services and property.

The rationale is to close infrastructure or project gaps by leveraging scarce public funding and introducing private technology and innovation to improve service quality and operational efficiency.

Benefits

  • Creative and innovative approaches to required outputs.
  • Integrated solutions to public needs.
  • Lower implementation costs and quality gains through economies of scale and efficiency.
  • Access to private technical, managerial, financial and technological resources.
  • Large capital injections while reducing public debt and aid dependency.
  • Better responsiveness to consumer needs.
  • Economic growth through investment and expanded public goods/services.
  • Appropriate allocation of risks and returns.

Examples and Areas of Application

Regional examples shown are DART in Tanzania, Nairobi Expressway in Kenya and Bujagali Hydro Plant in Uganda. Application areas include transport (roads, bridges, tunnels, airports, seaports, rail and transit), power and energy, telecommunications, healthcare, education and social infrastructure such as prisons and courthouses. The notes also group examples as transport infrastructure (highways, airports, railroads, bridges and tunnels), municipal and environmental infrastructure (water and wastewater facilities), and public service accommodations (school buildings, prisons, student dormitories, and entertainment or sports facilities).

PPP Delivery Models

ModelDescription
DBPrivate partner designs and builds to public specifications, often at a fixed price, and assumes risk.
O&MPrivate firm operates a publicly owned asset for a specified period; public partner retains ownership.
DBFOPrivate company designs, finances and constructs, operates/maintains under a long-term lease, then transfers the asset at lease end.
BOOPrivate party finances, builds, owns and operates perpetually subject to agreement and regulation.
BOOTPrivate party finances, designs, builds and operates/charges user fees for a specified period before transfer.
BBOPublic asset is legally transferred to a private partner for a designated period.
BLOTPrivate partner designs, finances and builds on leased public land, operates during the lease and transfers assets at expiry.
Operation LicensePrivate partner receives legal permission to operate a public service for a specified term; noted as common in IT projects.
Finance OnlyPrivate financial entity funds infrastructure and charges the public partner interest.

Challenges

  • Weak policy, legal and institutional frameworks.
  • Inadequate technical, socio-economic and commercial feasibility analysis.
  • Inadequate enabling environment, long-term finance and risk-sharing mechanisms.
  • Insufficient negotiation, procurement, implementation and management capacity.
  • Risk sharing that can leave the public sector with the full burden.
  • Low public awareness of PPP benefits.

How PPPs Work

A city government, for example, might be heavily indebted and unable to undertake a capital-intensive building project, but a private enterprise might be interested in funding its construction in exchange for receiving the operating profits once the project is complete. Contracts commonly last 20–30 years or longer. Financing may come partly from private partners and partly from public or user payments. The private partner may design, complete, implement and fund while the public partner defines objectives and monitors compliance. Risks are negotiated and ideally allocated according to each party's ability to assess, control and cope with them. Payments may come from public revenue (such as with hospital projects), direct user payments, shadow tolls based on actual usage, or user fees; where wastewater treatment is involved, payment is made from fees collected from users.

Advantages and Risks

  • Technology and innovation can improve operational efficiency.
  • Public incentives can support on-time and budget-conscious delivery.
  • Economic diversification can strengthen competitiveness and related industries.
  • Construction risk: delays, overruns or defects.
  • Availability risk: failure to provide promised services or meet standards.
  • Demand risk: fewer users than expected.
  • Public accountability risks, including substandard services or rights concerns.
  • Consumer pricing risks where users have limited alternatives.
  • Principal–agent problems, corruption, favoritism, rent-seeking and possible taxpayer liability.

PPP Framework and Spectrum

The PPP Framework visual groups conditions into Macro Factors (macroeconomic conditions, public financial management, governance/anti-corruption, FDI regime, investment climate and capital markets/long-term finance), Enabling Environment (political commitment, sector reform choices, PPP policy/strategy, regulatory/legal basis and institutional capacity), and Transaction Execution (governance and coordination, roles/PPP units, pipeline development, advisory/investor outreach, financial closure/guarantees and contract/performance monitoring).

The spectrum visual shows that PPP arrangements range across greater public to greater private participation and include conventional procurement, service and management arrangements, O&M, leases, concessions, privatization and infrastructure-investment models such as Build–Transfer–Operate, DBFO and BOO. The key lesson is that responsibility, ownership, risk and private involvement vary by arrangement.

LECTURE 6

Lecture 6: Financial Statement Analysis

Introduction

Financial analysis selects, evaluates and interprets financial data and other pertinent information to assess a company's present and future financial condition and performance. The introductory framework combines economic data, market data and financial disclosures.

Common-Size Analysis

Common-size analysis restates financial statement information in standardized form. Horizontal analysis uses a base-year amount and expresses later years as a percentage of it, helping compare growth over time. Vertical analysis uses the aggregate statement amount for the same year as the base: total assets for the balance sheet and revenues/sales for the income statement.

The CS Company example uses 2008–2013 assets (cash, inventory, receivables, net plant and equipment, intangibles and total assets). The source presents both the vertical percentages and horizontal percentages using 2008 as the base, demonstrating composition and relative growth.

Financial Ratio Analysis

Ratios use relationships among financial statement accounts to assess condition and performance. The categories are activity, liquidity, solvency and profitability.

SolvencyAbility to satisfy debt obligations.
ProfitabilityAbility to manage expenses and produce profits from sales.

DuPont Analysis

The DuPont framework decomposes return using financial statement relationships. The three-factor ROE form uses total asset turnover, financial leverage and net profit margin. The five-factor form uses total asset turnover, financial leverage, operating profit margin, effect of nonoperating items and tax effect.

The D Company example shows ROE declining from 0.22 to 0.20 while ROA, asset turnover and net profit margin increased; financial leverage fell from 2.00 to 1.60. The lesson is that decomposition identifies the underlying source of a change rather than relying on one overall ratio.

Other and Shareholder Ratios

  • EPS = Net income available to common shareholders ÷ common shares outstanding.
  • Basic EPS uses net income after preferred dividends and average common shares.
  • Diluted EPS considers all dilutive securities.
  • Book value per share = book value of equity ÷ number of shares.
  • P/E = price per share ÷ EPS; last four quarters produce trailing P/E.
  • DPS = dividends paid ÷ weighted average ordinary shares.
  • Dividend payout = dividends to common shareholders ÷ net income attributable to common shares.
  • Plowback ratio = 1 − dividend payout ratio, representing retained earnings proportion.

The shareholder example produces book value per share of $1.00, P/E of 16.67, DPS of $0.12, payout of 40% and plowback of 60%, with interpretations given in the notes.

Effective Use and Pro Forma Analysis

Ratios should be interpreted over time, against companies in the same line of business and in the context of company events, accounting changes, product mix, industry information and major influences.

  1. Estimate typical relationships between revenues and sales-driven accounts.
  2. Estimate fixed burdens such as interest and taxes.
  3. Forecast revenues.
  4. Estimate sales-driven accounts from forecast revenues.
  5. Estimate fixed burdens.
  6. Construct future-period income statements and balance sheets.

The Imaginaire Company example forecasts 5% sales growth, COGS at 60% of revenue, SG&A at 10%, interest at 8% of long-term debt, taxes at 35% of earnings before taxes and a 50% dividend payout. Its balance-sheet example forecasts current assets at 60% of revenues, net plant/equipment at 100%, current liabilities at 25%, debt to maintain capital structure and retained earnings based on prior retained earnings plus net income less dividends.

The source summary connects historical understanding (common-size and ratios) with future forecasting (pro forma analysis).
Completeness Addition · Financial Statement Analysis

Financial Analysis: Additional Ratio and Example Details Retained from the Source

The final audit found that the main handbook already covered the major ratio groups and DuPont analysis, but several specific source items needed to be stated explicitly to avoid losing distinct concepts.

Additional Solvency Coverage Ratios

  • Interest coverage ratio = EBIT ÷ Interest payments: ability to satisfy interest obligations.
  • Fixed charge coverage ratio = (EBIT + Lease payments) ÷ (Interest payments + Lease payments): ability to satisfy interest and lease obligations.
  • Cash flow coverage ratio = (CFO + Interest payments + Tax payments) ÷ Interest payments: ability to satisfy interest obligations using cash flows.
  • Cash-flow-to-debt ratio = CFO ÷ Total debt: indicates the length of time needed to pay debt using cash flows.

Other Per-Share and Market Ratios Named in the Notes

  • Earnings per share (EPS): net income available to common shareholders divided by the number of common shares outstanding.
  • Basic EPS: net income after preferred dividends divided by average common shares outstanding.
  • Diluted EPS: considers all dilutive securities when determining the number of shares.
  • Book value per share: book value of equity divided by the number of shares.
  • Price-to-earnings (P/E) ratio: price per share divided by earnings per share.

DuPont Formula Build-Up (Source Equation Sequence)

The notes present the DuPont decomposition as a sequence of equations, expanding return on equity step by step:

  • Two-component form: Return on equity = (Total assets ÷ Shareholders' equity) × Return on assets.
  • Restated: Return on equity = (Total assets ÷ Shareholders' equity) × (Net income ÷ Total assets).
  • Three-component form: Return on equity = (Total assets ÷ Shareholders' equity) × (Revenues ÷ Total assets) × (Net income ÷ Revenues) — i.e., financial leverage × total asset turnover × net profit margin.
  • Five-component form: the net profit margin term is further decomposed into operating profit margin, effect of nonoperating items and tax effect, giving financial leverage × total asset turnover × operating profit margin × effect of nonoperating items × tax effect.

Source DuPont Example

The notes provide a D Company comparison in which return on equity declined from 0.22 in 2012 to 0.20 in 2013. The source table reports return on assets rising from 0.11 to 0.13 while financial leverage falls from 2.00 to 1.60, alongside changes in total asset turnover, margins, nonoperating effects and tax effect. The teaching purpose is to decompose a change in ROE rather than interpret one ratio in isolation.

Source Pro Forma Examples

The Imaginaire Company example forecasts 5% revenue growth and then constructs a future income statement and balance sheet using relationships stated in the notes: COGS at 60% of revenue, SG&A at 10%, interest at 8% of long-term debt, taxes at 35% of earnings before taxes and a 50% dividend payout. The balance-sheet illustration links current assets to 60% of revenues, net plant and equipment to 100%, current liabilities to 25%, debt to the capital structure and retained earnings to prior retained earnings plus net income less dividends.

Interpretation preserved from the notes: common-size and ratio analysis help explain historical financial condition and performance; pro forma analysis uses relationships among financial statement accounts to forecast likely future performance.
LECTURE 7

Lecture 7: Project Finance

Meaning and Structure

Project finance funds long-term infrastructure, industrial projects and public services using non-recourse or limited-recourse structures. Debt and equity are repaid primarily from project cash flow, with project assets, rights and interests serving as secondary collateral. The notes also describe the off-balance-sheet attraction and explain that under a non-recourse loan a debtor cannot be pursued beyond seizure of the asset.

Parties

  • Sponsor/equity investor
  • Senior and/or mezzanine lenders
  • Contractor and equipment supplier
  • Operator
  • Financial advisors
  • Technical advisors
  • Legal advisors
  • Equity investors
  • Regulatory agencies
  • Multilateral agencies/export credit agencies
  • Insurance providers
  • Hedge providers

Contractual Framework

  • Shareholder/sponsor documents
  • Project documents
  • Finance documents
  • Security documents
  • Other project documents
  • Director/promoter contribution

Types and Sources of Finance

Private finance may use retained earnings, equity partners, bonds, new stocks or borrowing; potential providers include pension funds, insurance companies, investment trusts and commercial banks. Public finance may use tax receipts, general-revenue bonds, special bonds with dedicated income and government grants. Sources listed include general/special-purpose bonds, tax revenues, capital grants/subsidies and international subsidized loans.

Public–private partnerships are also presented as a financing type and connect directly to Lecture 5.

Benefits

  • Can avoid a corporate repayment guarantee and support off-balance-sheet financing.
  • Can extend sponsor debt capacity and raise funding based on contractual commitments.
  • Allows sponsors to share risks through security arrangements, contractual agreements and supplemental credit support.
  • Allows fund providers to decide how to manage free cash flow after operating, maintenance and statutory payments.
  • May protect valuable information and competitive advantage, particularly for equity finance, though this is more limited for project bonds.

Project Cost Estimate, Budget and Cost Baseline

The Surveyor Pro figures illustrate a project cost estimate, a software development estimate and a project cost baseline. They show costs being broken into work breakdown structure items and estimates, then allocated over a baseline for monitoring.

A project budget is the total projected cost needed over a defined period and includes labour, material procurement and operating costs. It helps estimate total costs before completion and monitor whether the project remains within budget.

Cost Budgeting and Cost Control

Cost budgeting allocates project cost estimates to individual work items and provides a cost baseline. The example refers to a Business Systems Replacement project with $600,000 of purchased cost estimates and $1.2 million for Information Services and Technology, allocated to appropriate budgets.

  • Monitor cost performance.
  • Ensure only appropriate changes enter a revised cost baseline.
  • Inform stakeholders about authorized changes affecting costs.
  • Use earned value management as an important cost-control tool.

Rate of Performance

Rate of performance (RP) is actual work completed relative to the percentage of work planned at a given time. The source example: if server installation is 50% complete when the schedule expected 100%, RP is 50% (50/100).

LECTURE 8

Lecture 8: Project Budgeting

Discussion Questions in the Notes

  • What is project budgeting?
  • Explain the project budgeting process in your organization.
  • What are the challenges?
  • Recommend improvements.

Meaning and Importance

Project budgeting translates a project plan into fiscal terms: how much completion will cost, where money will come from and how it will be used. The budget must reflect and support the project narrative. A high-quality budget is a complete, accurate outline of project costs and their eligibility. Unlike an organizational operating budget for annual revenues and expenses, a project budget covers costs associated with a specific funded project period.

  • Funders judge implementation capability through the budget.
  • Mistakes can create professional and legal commitments with inadequate resources.
  • A high-quality budget can improve approval chances, support implementation, speed accurate reporting and show funders what they support.

Budget Basics and Expense Categories

  • Allocate funds for planning/development, implementation and evaluation.
  • Allocate for people, places and things.
  • Distinguish start-up from operating costs.
  • Distinguish direct costs from indirect costs; indirect costs support the host organization and can be allocated appropriately.

How to Prepare a Project Proposal Budget

  1. Understand funder requirements and required format; when possible, align with the chart of accounts for easier reporting.
  2. Work with teammates to determine anticipated needs and direct/indirect costs using realistic numbers.
  3. Review the narrative, list all anticipated costs and include in-kind costs such as volunteers, books and materials.
  4. Consider payroll, materials, travel, events, catering and overlooked items such as volunteer gifts or speaker honoraria.
  5. Ensure every narrative item with a cost is reflected in the budget.

Planning and Development Process

  • Determine the project.
  • Establish period and timeframe.
  • Identify total costs including personnel, travel and equipment.
  • Arrange items by category and anticipated cost and determine indirect costs.
  • Value donated goods/services using real costs and valid sources.
  • Transfer data into the funder-required format.
  • Expect meetings, estimates from vendors and review of past expense reports.

Budget Justification, Considerations and Narrative

A budget justification explains costs for people or tasks, provides a rationale for allocations and documents how costs were derived.

  • Research costs for realism.
  • Research the funder's available amount, award limits and prior funding patterns.
  • Consider asking no more than an average project amount where relevant.
  • Review, revise and refine continuously.
  • Specific rather than round amounts may appear more authentic.
  • The narrative expands line items and ties costs to activities and goals; it should be clear and concise.
  • Review similar budget narratives to understand expectations.

Red Flags and Final Checklist

  • Narrative must match line items exactly.
  • Total amount should be commensurate with proposed outcomes.
  • Use fair and reasonable costs.
  • Tell the same story as the proposal narrative.
  • Be sufficient for all tasks.
  • Avoid unexplained amounts or miscellaneous categories.
  • Include only costs incurred during the project period.
  • Follow funder guidelines.

PROCUREMENT TOPIC

Principles of Public Procurement and Procurement for NGOs

Importance of Public Procurement Principles

Applying procurement principles supports efficient and responsible public-resource use, reduces fraud and corruption opportunities, promotes fair competition, increases public confidence and helps obtain quality goods, works and services at the best overall value.

Ten Principles of Public Procurement

PrincipleCore meaning and source example
TransparencyProcesses are open, clear and documented; opportunities, criteria and awards are accessible. Example: a ministry publicly advertises a tender and states selection criteria.
AccountabilityOfficials are responsible for decisions and must justify them during audits/reviews. Example: complete supplier-selection and award records.
Fairness and Equal TreatmentEligible suppliers receive the same information and opportunity; decisions use objective criteria. Example: same scoring system for every bidder.
CompetitionMultiple suppliers compete on price, quality and service. Example: multiple qualified contractors are invited rather than unjustified direct award.
Value for MoneyOptimal balance of cost, quality, reliability and lifetime performance; lowest price is not always best.
Integrity and Ethical ConductHonesty and ethical conduct; prevent bribery, fraud, favoritism and conflicts of interest.
Efficiency and EconomyTimely, cost-effective procurement while meeting quality/performance standards.
ConfidentialityProtect sensitive information such as pre-opening bid prices and evaluation deliberations.
Non-DiscriminationDo not exclude suppliers on irrelevant grounds unless law/policy permits.
Compliance with Laws and RegulationsFollow applicable laws, regulations, policies and procedures.

NGO Procurement

NGO procurement acquires goods, works and services while ensuring value for money, transparency, fairness, accountability and compliance with donor and organizational policies. It helps NGOs use resources efficiently and maintain public and donor trust.

Objectives

  • Obtain quality goods/services at competitive prices.
  • Ensure transparency and accountability.
  • Promote fair supplier competition.
  • Comply with donor, legal and organizational requirements.
  • Prevent fraud, corruption and conflicts of interest.
  • Ensure timely delivery.

Principles for NGOs

  • Transparency: documented and open to review.
  • Fair competition: equal opportunities for qualified suppliers.
  • Value for money: balance cost, quality, delivery and service.
  • Accountability: responsibility for decisions and fund use.
  • Integrity: ethical conduct and freedom from conflicts of interest.
  • Efficiency: timely and cost-effective acquisition.

Procurement Process

  1. Identify the need.
  2. Prepare specifications for quality, quantity and technical requirements.
  3. Develop a procurement plan and estimate costs.
  4. Invite quotations or tenders based on procurement value.
  5. Evaluate bids using predefined criteria such as price, quality, experience and delivery time.
  6. Award the contract.
  7. Issue a purchase order or sign a contract.
  8. Receive and inspect goods/services.
  9. Process payment after satisfactory delivery.
  10. Maintain complete records for audit and reporting.

Common Methods

MethodTypical use
Petty Cash PurchaseLow-value, urgent purchases
Request for Quotations (RFQ)Medium-value procurement with quotations from multiple suppliers
Open TenderHigh-value procurement requiring broad competition
Restricted TenderLimited number of qualified suppliers
Single-Source ProcurementJustified cases such as emergencies or one available supplier

Key Documents

  • Procurement plan
  • Purchase requisition
  • Technical specifications or Terms of Reference
  • RFQ or Invitation to Tender
  • Bid evaluation report
  • Purchase order or contract
  • Delivery note
  • Goods Received Note
  • Supplier invoice
  • Payment voucher

Challenges and Best Practices

  • Challenges: limited funding, supplier delays, price fluctuations, weak supplier performance, fraud/corruption/conflicts and multiple donor rules.
  • Best practices: written policies, segregation of duties, competitive quotations, complete audit records, supplier evaluation, staff training and monitoring donor/legal compliance.

Source Example

An NGO in Arusha needs 50 desks for a community learning center at an estimated cost of TZS 15 million. The notes show a sequence of specifications, at least three qualified quotations, evaluation by price/quality/delivery/warranty, award based on best overall value and inspection before payment. The example demonstrates transparency, value for money and compliance.
